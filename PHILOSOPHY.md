# SuperBasic™ Research Philosophy
## The Spirit Behind the System

---

## Preface: A Confession

This document is unashamedly idealistic. It asks unanswered questions.
It dwells in impracticality. It refuses to accept that "everyone does it
this way" is sufficient justification for anything. If you came here
looking for tactical checklists, you're in the wrong place — though
you'll find those in `references/`. This is the manifesto. This is the
WHY.

These standards were built out of frustration. Frustration with business
books that made confident assertions based on cherry-picked anecdotes.
Frustration with sales methodologies that confused tactics with
strategy. Frustration with research reports that buried their
limitations in footnotes while shouting their conclusions from
headlines. Frustration with living in an age where information is
infinite but trust is scarce, where anyone can publish anything and the
reader is left to figure out what's actually true.

Stefan P, who built SuperBasic™, studied law and history before
stumbling into business. Law teaches you to interrogate sources: Who
wrote this? Why? What were they trying to achieve? History teaches you
that every document is propaganda for something, and your job is to
figure out what. Business, unfortunately, often teaches you to ignore
these questions in favor of "move fast and break things."

This philosophy is what happens when you refuse that lesson.

## Part I: The Problem

### The Misinformation Age Isn't What You Think

We don't live in an information shortage. We live in a signal shortage.
The problem isn't that information doesn't exist — the problem is that
*too much* information exists, most of it contradictory, much of it
self-serving, and none of it labeled with the context required to
evaluate its credibility.

Consider a simple research question: *"Is this firm prepared for a new
compliance regulation?"*

Answering this means wading through the firm's own website claiming
"comprehensive readiness," press releases announcing "strategic
partnerships" with vendors, job postings that may or may not signal
actual preparation, industry association reports funded by vendors
selling compliance solutions, journalism aggregating other journalism
aggregating press releases, social posts from consultants selling
compliance services, anonymous employee reviews that may be competitors
planting doubt, analyst reports written by firms paid by the companies
they analyze, academic papers that are theoretically sound but
practically outdated, and thought-leadership content that mixes genuine
insight with lead generation.

**Every single one of these sources has an agenda.** Even the "neutral"
ones. Especially the neutral ones.

The traditional research approach says: "Gather sources, synthesize
insights, make recommendations." But this assumes sources are roughly
equivalent in quality and motivation, that more sources = more truth,
and that synthesis is a straightforward process of averaging
perspectives.

**This is catastrophically wrong.**

Ten promotional vendor white papers synthesized into a "comprehensive
market overview" isn't research — it's aggregated marketing. Five
journalistic articles all citing the same company press release isn't
triangulation — it's echo-chamber amplification. Three expert opinions
from consultants who all work for firms selling the same solutions isn't
diversity — it's monoculture with business cards.

We live in an age where the appearance of research has become easier
than ever, while the reality of rigorous research has become harder than
ever. Anyone can quote sources. Few people interrogate them.

### The Standards Crisis

The collapse of research standards isn't limited to business. Academia
has its replication crisis. Journalism has its wire-service dependency.
Think tanks have their funding-transparency problems. Every field that
produces "knowledge" is struggling with the same fundamental question:
**How do we know what we claim to know?**

The business world's response has largely been to pretend the question
doesn't exist. Consultants deliver recommendations with confidence that
far exceeds their evidence base. Market research firms publish reports
that are substantially vendor-funded content with a thin veneer of
"independent analysis." Business books cite anecdotes as proof while
carefully avoiding the companies that tried the same approach and
failed.

The implicit deal is: *We all pretend certainty exists, readers don't
ask uncomfortable questions about methodology, and everyone moves
forward with plausible deniability.*

**SuperBasic™ is a rejection of this deal.**

We start from a different premise: certainty doesn't exist in business
research. But intellectual honesty does. And rigorous methodology does.
And transparent documentation does.

If you can't be certain, you can at least be honest about your evidence
base and clear about your limitations. This turns out to be far more
valuable than false confidence based on thin foundations.

## Part II: The Philosophical Foundation

### First Principles: The Historiographic Method

Studying history teaches you something critical: **every document is an
artifact of human motivation**. The medieval chronicle wasn't
"history" — it was propaganda for a king. The company annual report
isn't "truth" — it's a legal requirement crafted by people with specific
incentives. The expert opinion isn't "objective analysis" — it's a
perspective shaped by professional incentives, cognitive biases, and the
funding that pays for that expertise.

Historiography — the study of how history is written — teaches you to
ask:

1. **Who created this source?** (Proximity to events, access to
   information, position in power structures)
2. **When was it created?** (Contemporary to events or retrospective?
   Before or after the outcome was known?)
3. **Why was it created?** (Legal requirement, promotional intent,
   personal memoir, political justification)
4. **For whom was it created?** (Internal document, public relations,
   peer communication, regulatory filing)
5. **What could they know?** (Direct observation, hearsay, inference,
   speculation)
6. **What did they want?** (Preservation of reputation, justification of
   decisions, manipulation of perception)

These aren't cynical questions — they're *essential* questions. And they
apply as much to a social media post today as to a document written
centuries ago.

**Source Scoring is historiography for business research.** The
dimensions in `references/source-scoring.md` operationalize these
eternal questions into a reproducible evaluation framework.

### The Law School Method: Interrogate Everything

Legal training drills one habit into you: **never accept a claim without
interrogating its foundation**. If a witness testifies, you ask: How do
they know? What's their relationship to the parties? What's their
incentive to testify this way? If a document is introduced, you ask:
Who created it? When? Why? Can it be authenticated?

This isn't paranoia — it's discipline. The courtroom stakes (someone's
freedom, property, or life) demand it. Business stakes are lower, but
the principle remains: **if you're making important decisions based on
information, you need to know whether that information is trustworthy.**

The problem with most business research is that it treats sources like
facts. "According to [analyst firm]..." becomes the end of inquiry
rather than the beginning. But an analyst firm is a company with a
business model — that doesn't make it worthless, it makes it *a
specific type of source with specific strengths and specific
limitations*.

**Source Personas emerged from this instinct to categorize sources by
their relationship to truth.** The Primary Artifact (direct evidence of
action), the Independent Observer (external verification), the Captured
Expert (aggregated but funded), the Promotional Insider (self-interested
but informative) — these aren't arbitrary labels. They're recognition
patterns for source reliability in adversarial evaluation. See
`references/source-personas.md`.

### The First-Principles Rebellion

A simple realization drove this: **most business books are just
consulting pitches with extra pages**. They present frameworks as if
they're natural laws rather than tactical choices shaped by the author's
practice area and client base. They cite "research" that turns out to be
the author's anecdotal client work. They cherry-pick success stories
while ignoring failed implementations.

This isn't malicious — it's structural. The business book exists in a
market. The market rewards confidence and simplicity. Nuance doesn't
sell. Admitting uncertainty doesn't get speaking gigs.

But **good research must resist market incentives that distort
truth-seeking.**

First-principles thinking asks: What's actually true here, not what's
marketable? What can we know, not what would sound impressive? What are
the limitations, not what can we hide in footnotes?

This has practical implications:

- **Bronze-tier reports are acceptable if limitations are disclosed.**
  We don't pretend everything is Gold Tier.
- **Triangulation matters more than volume.** Three independent sources
  from different personas beat thirty sources from the same
  perspective.
- **Time-appropriateness beats recency dogma.** "Recent" means different
  things for regulatory implementation versus cultural values. We match
  timeframes to subject tempo (see `references/decay-classes.md`), not
  arbitrary rules.
- **Persona diversity prevents blind spots.** Every source type sees
  reality differently. No single perspective suffices.

These aren't arbitrary methodological choices. They're **consequences of
taking epistemology seriously in a business context.**

## Part III: The Spirit and the Body

### Why "Rigorous But Practical" Isn't a Contradiction

The SuperBasic™ philosophy rests on a framework we call **Spirit and
Body** — a recognition that research operates in two registers
simultaneously.

**The Spirit:** The commitment to truth-seeking, intellectual honesty,
and rigorous evaluation. The refusal to cut corners or accept easy
answers. The discipline to document limitations rather than hide them.
The courage to say "I don't know" when evidence is insufficient.

**The Body:** The practical execution of research in time-constrained,
resource-limited, real-world contexts. The recognition that perfect
certainty is impossible and perfect comprehensiveness is unaffordable.
The wisdom to know when "good enough with disclosed limitations" is
actually the best possible outcome.

Lesser methodologies treat these as opposing forces: "Be rigorous"
versus "Be practical," as if you must choose. This creates the familiar
pattern where rigor gets sacrificed for speed, or where "practical"
becomes an excuse for sloppiness.

**SuperBasic™ rejects this false dichotomy.**

Rigor without practicality produces research that takes months and costs
a fortune for a question that needed an answer in two weeks. This is
waste.

Practicality without rigor produces fast, cheap research that reaches
confident conclusions from thin evidence. This is dangerous.

**The integration looks like this:**

**Spirit:** Every source must be scored. No exceptions.
**Body:** Scoring takes a few minutes per source once you've learned the
system. This isn't bureaucratic burden — it's quality control that
becomes muscle memory.

**Spirit:** Every key claim must be triangulated across multiple
independent sources from different personas.
**Body:** "Key claim" is defined narrowly — the assertions the report
depends on — so you're not triangulating background context.

**Spirit:** Report quality must be assessed across five dimensions
before delivery.
**Body:** This assessment takes fifteen to twenty minutes and often
reveals gaps that would've caused real disputes later. It's not
overhead — it's insurance.

The union of spirit and body produces **research that is both defensible
and deliverable.** Defensible because the methodology is explicit and
reproducible. Deliverable because the methodology is efficient enough to
use under real constraints.

### The Honest Broker Stance

SuperBasic™ research operates from a specific philosophical position:
**we are honest brokers between the reader and reality.**

This has implications:

**We serve the reader, but we don't serve their preferences.** If they
want to hear "Competitor X is failing," but our research shows
Competitor X is actually thriving, we report the truth. Research exists
to reduce uncertainty, not confirm biases.

**We acknowledge limitations explicitly.** If we only have Bronze-tier
sources in a particular area, we say so. If a key claim rests on a
single source, we flag it. Hiding limitations isn't service — it's
fraud.

**We distinguish between evidence and inference.** "The company posted
12 compliance job openings" is evidence. "The company is serious about
compliance" is inference from that evidence. We make this distinction
visible so a reader can judge whether our inferences are warranted.

**We price honesty appropriately.** A Gold-tier finding costs more to
produce than a Bronze-tier one because it requires more sources, more
time, more triangulation. Choosing a tier is a real choice with a real
cost — not a marketing category.

This stance is uncomfortable in contexts where "perception is reality."
But **research cannot function if truth is negotiable.**

The reader gets to decide whether our findings matter for their
decision. They don't get to decide whether our findings are supported
by evidence.

## Part IV: How We Got Here

### The Origin Story: Frustration as Methodology

SuperBasic™ didn't emerge from academic theory or consulting
methodology. It emerged from **rage at bad research claiming to be good
research.**

The pattern, over and over: a "market analysis" deliverable that
collected public information, repeated company claims, cited press
releases as evidence, and made confident recommendations based on this
foundation of aspiration and marketing.

When pressed — "How do we know this is true?" — the answer was usually:
"Well, multiple sources confirm it."

Investigation revealed: multiple sources were all journalists citing the
same press release. Or multiple analysts repeating the same
vendor-funded research. Or multiple posts echoing the same thought
leader.

**This wasn't triangulation. This was echo-chamber amplification with
citation formatting.**

The frustration led to questions: Why don't we have explicit standards
for source quality? Why don't we distinguish between source types
systematically? Why don't we document what we don't know as carefully as
what we do know? Why is the default assumption "more sources = better
research" rather than "independent sources = better research"?

The answers were unsatisfying: "That's how everyone does it." "Nobody
asks for that level of rigor." "It would take too long."

**SuperBasic™ was built in rejection of these answers.**

### The Development Process: Trial, Error, and Refinement

The first version of the scoring system was too complex — more
dimensions than agents could apply consistently, with scoring criteria
that required judgment calls no rubric can standardize. It worked
beautifully in theory and failed completely in practice.

The solution: reduce to the essential dimensions, write clearer scoring
criteria with examples, test with real research, measure agreement
between independent scorers, refine based on where disagreements
emerged.

The persona framework emerged later, when patterns showed up in how
sources scored: job postings score high on proximity and verifiability
but low on independence. Vendor white papers score medium on proximity,
high on verifiability, low on independence. Anonymous reviews score low
on proximity and verifiability but high on independence.

The numerical patterns were consistent, but the *meaning* of those
patterns required contextual understanding. A Gold-tier job posting
tells you operational priorities. A Bronze-tier vendor white paper tells
you the solutions landscape. A Questionable-tier anonymous review tells
you cultural signals — if you look at patterns across many reviews
rather than individual claims.

**The personas gave names and characters to these patterns.** This
transformed source evaluation from mechanical scoring into skilled
judgment: score the source for quality, then recognize its persona to
understand its purpose.

The report-scoring framework came last, from asking: **what makes a
research report actually trustworthy?** Diversity prevents
single-perspective blindness. Quality prevents weak-source dependency.
Triangulation prevents single-source risk. Recency prevents outdated
conclusions. Depth prevents claims outrunning evidence. These aren't
theoretical — they're the patterns found in failed research.

### The Continuous Refinement Principle

SuperBasic™ Research Standards aren't finished. They're a continuous
refinement process.

The philosophy rarely changes — first principles are stable. But the
operational frameworks (scoring criteria, persona descriptions,
reference tables) update based on field experience: when agents
consistently struggle with a scoring dimension, criteria get clarified;
when new source types emerge, evaluation guidelines get developed; when
reference tables don't match real-world complexity, nuance gets added.

**The commitment is to methodology improvement, not methodology
preservation.** If evidence shows a better way, the way changes. The
spirit stays constant. The body evolves.

## Part V: The Stakes

### Why This Matters: The Cost of Bad Research

Bad research has real consequences. Organizations make wrong strategic
decisions. Investments go to businesses based on fabricated market
opportunities. Competitive positioning strategies rest on
misunderstandings of competitor capabilities.

But the costs are often invisible because the research-to-outcome chain
is long, failures are attributed to execution rather than analysis, and
nobody audits research methodology after the fact.

**SuperBasic™ makes this cost visible through documentation.** When you
deliver a Bronze-tier finding with disclosed limitations and someone
proceeds anyway, then the outcome disappoints, you can point to: "We
told you the evidence was thin here. Here's the source library showing
the gaps. You chose to proceed with known uncertainty."

This isn't blame-shifting — it's **appropriate risk communication.** The
reader deserves to know what they're buying. Bronze-tier research is
legitimate research if the reader understands what Bronze tier means.

The danger is Gold-tier presentation of Bronze-tier research. That's
malpractice.

### The Trust Crisis: Why Methodology Matters

We live in an era of collapsed trust in expertise. This isn't
irrational — experts have been wrong, repeatedly, on high-stakes
questions.

**The problem isn't that experts make mistakes — it's that experts claim
certainty they don't possess.**

SuperBasic™ offers a different model: **transparent methodology as the
basis for trust.**

We don't ask a reader to trust us because we're experts. We ask them to
trust us because we show our work, disclose limitations, use
reproducible methods, and distinguish evidence from inference. This
isn't trust based on authority. It's trust based on **verification.**

## Part VI: The Limitations (Yes, Even Philosophies Have Them)

### What SuperBasic™ Can't Do

**It cannot eliminate uncertainty.** Decisions require judgment under
uncertainty. The best research reduces uncertainty but never eliminates
it.

**It cannot make bad sources good.** If the information simply doesn't
exist, no methodology will conjure it. Sometimes the honest answer is
"we can't answer this with available sources." That's a finding, not a
failure.

**It cannot prevent motivated reasoning.** Researchers are human. The
methodology creates guardrails but doesn't eliminate the risk of
cherry-picking or interpreting evidence favorably. Vigilance is still
required.

**It cannot replace domain expertise.** The scoring system tells you
*that* a source has high expertise. It doesn't give you the expertise to
*evaluate* whether that expertise is being applied well.

### The Meta-Question: How Do We Know This Methodology Is Right?

If SuperBasic™ is built on skepticism of sources, shouldn't we be
skeptical of SuperBasic™ itself?

**Yes. Absolutely yes.**

This methodology emerged from specific frustrations applied to specific
contexts by people with specific training. It's not divinely revealed
truth.

**The justification for SuperBasic™ isn't that it's perfect — it's
that it's:**

1. **Explicit** — anyone can read the methodology and apply it
2. **Reproducible** — two researchers scoring the same source should get
   similar results
3. **Improvable** — field evidence can refine the framework
4. **Honest** — it acknowledges its own limitations

Is there a better methodology? Possibly. If you find one, that's worth
knowing.

### The Danger of Methodology Worship

The greatest risk to SuperBasic™ is that it becomes **a bureaucratic
ritual rather than a quality tool** — sources scored mechanically
without thinking about what the scores mean, checklists completed pro
forma, documentation as box-checking rather than actual audit trail.

**The methodology is a tool. The goal is truth-seeking.** When
methodology becomes the goal itself, quality dies.

## Part VII: The Invitation

SuperBasic™ is a rejection of research-as-marketing,
research-as-authority-performance, and research-as-confidence-without-
foundation.

It's a commitment to rigorous evaluation over lazy acceptance,
transparent methodology over expert mystique, disclosed limitations over
false certainty, triangulated evidence over single-source dependency,
appropriate doubt over confident ignorance.

This isn't comfortable. Markets reward simplicity over nuance. Culture
favors "move fast" over "evaluate carefully."

**SuperBasic™ says: the stakes are too high to care.**

If your research informs real decisions, you owe it to intellectual
honesty to do the work properly — spend the time to score sources
rigorously, seek counter-evidence, find the courage to say "our evidence
is thin here," and build the documentation that proves you did the work.

**Not perfection. Not certainty. Not authority.**

**Rigor. Honesty. Transparency.**
