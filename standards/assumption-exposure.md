# Assumption Exposure: Research Validity Reference Guide
**Hidden Assumptions That Can Reverse Your Findings**

---

## What Is an Assumption?

An **assumption** is something your research report asserts as true —
explicitly or implicitly — without direct verification. Every research
claim rests on unstated preconditions. Miss one, and your entire
conclusion collapses.

**Example:**
- **Claim:** "Company X is confident about its market position"
- **Assumption:** Aggressive hiring = confidence (not panic, not
  acquisition prep, not mandatory headcount replacement)
- **If wrong:** The claim flips. Hiring signals threat, not confidence.

**Why expose them?**
Assumptions aren't failures — they're unavoidable. No research verifies
everything. But **hidden assumptions undermine credibility**. A reader
acts on your report, then discovers you assumed something without
stating it. Trust breaks. Your findings become suspect.

Exposed assumptions do the opposite: they show your rigor. They signal:
"We know what we don't know, and here's how it affects the conclusion."
That transparency builds confidence even when findings are uncertain.

**What happens if you don't list them?**
- Reader discovers an assumption → questions the whole report
- Assumption proves wrong → report is discredited retroactively
- A critic cites the report against you → credibility damage spreads
- Future research inherits unstated assumptions → compound errors
  accumulate

## Five Categories of Research Assumptions

Research assumptions cluster into five categories. Most reports contain
at least one from each group.

### 1. Source Assumptions
**"The source is truthful / complete / unbiased"**

These assumptions are about the **reliability and incentive structure**
of your sources.

| Assumption | Example | Red Flag | Impact if Wrong |
|-----------|---------|----------|-----------------|
| **Source truthfulness** | "We assume Company A's job board accurately reflects current hiring" | Source has incentive to misrepresent (attract/deter candidates) | All claims sourced to A become suspect |
| **Source completeness** | "We assume LinkedIn's hiring data captures 80%+ of actual hires" | LinkedIn skews toward certain industries/geographies | Sample is systematically biased |
| **Source recency** | "We assume this year's sources reflect current policy" | Policy changed since the source was published | Recommendation based on outdated state |
| **Source independence** | "We assume News Article B independently investigated" | Article is actually citing Press Release A | Multiple sources collapse into one |
| **Source expertise** | "We assume the industry analyst has current market knowledge" | Analyst hasn't worked in field for years | Recommendations lag reality |

**Common source assumption errors:**
- Using company press releases as "independent verification"
- Trusting news articles that cite the same press release (false
  triangulation)
- Assuming old sources are still valid if they're "foundational
  research"
- Treating archived data as current (profiles change)
- Assuming silence on a topic means the source didn't find anything
  (vs. didn't look)

---

### 2. Causality Assumptions
**"X causes Y (not the reverse, not a third factor)"**

Causality assumptions are the **trickiest**. Human brains are
pattern-matching machines. We see correlation and assume causation.
Research must defend against this bias.

| Assumption | Example | Red Flag | Impact if Wrong |
|-----------|---------|----------|-----------------|
| **Direction of causality** | "Hiring increases = confidence (not panic-driven or acquisition-prep hiring)" | No direct evidence of motive; multiple motives possible | Entire competitive threat assessment flips |
| **Single vs. multiple causes** | "Regulatory change caused a compliance hiring surge (not talent shortage, not market expansion)" | Multiple factors coincide; labor market also tight | Attribution is wrong; recommendations miss actual drivers |
| **Magnitude of causation** | "Social media presence is THE key driver of brand perception (not price, not product quality)" | No weighting analysis; assumed dominance untested | Strategic priorities misaligned with actual levers |
| **Excluded factors** | "Market share decline is due to competitor strategy (not internal execution, not market shift)" | Didn't investigate internal factors; external attribution convenient | Recommendations don't address root cause |

**Common causality errors:**
- Seeing "both things increased" and concluding one caused the other
- Assuming a company statement about motivation = proof of motivation
- Treating an industry trend as proof of an individual company's trend
- Ignoring confounding variables that explain the pattern better
- Assuming a rare event has a rare cause rather than a common one (e.g.
  budget cycle)

---

### 3. Scope Assumptions
**"This finding applies to [geography / segment / time period / use
case]"**

Scope assumptions define **the boundaries of validity**. Violate them,
and your findings may not apply.

| Assumption | Example | Red Flag | Impact if Wrong |
|-----------|---------|----------|-----------------|
| **Geographic scope** | "National hiring patterns apply to the whole region" | Regulations differ by country; labor markets differ | Recommendations work in one place, fail elsewhere |
| **Segment scope** | "Large-firm patterns apply to mid-market" | Resources scale differently below a certain size | Strategy won't work without adaptation |
| **Time period scope** | "Current market conditions persist through next quarter" | Hiring patterns are cyclical; downturn possible | Forecast based on assumption, not trend |
| **Industry scope** | "Insights from one regulated sector apply to another" | Regulatory scrutiny differs by sector | Recommendations overestimate or underestimate a real burden |
| **Use case scope** | "This data supports hiring strategy (not pricing strategy)" | Study measured recruitment, not customer sensitivity | Using findings in wrong context |

**Common scope errors:**
- Generalizing from one country/market to the world
- Scaling insights from large companies down to small ones without an
  adjustment factor
- Assuming current trend continues without seasonal/cyclical analysis
- Using competitor patterns as if they apply to the subject directly
- Applying findings to a decision outside the original research scope

---

### 4. Market Assumptions
**"The market structure / competitive dynamics / customer behavior is
[as we assume]"**

Market assumptions are about **how the market works**, not what's
happening in it.

| Assumption | Example | Red Flag | Impact if Wrong |
|-----------|---------|----------|-----------------|
| **Market structure** | "The market is competitive (not monopolistic / duopolistic / highly fragmented)" | No analysis of competitor concentration | Competitive strategies won't work in duopoly |
| **Customer behavior** | "Customers prioritize price (not quality / brand / convenience)" | Assumption based on logic, not customer research | Recommendations optimize wrong variable |
| **Barrier to entry** | "Market entry is difficult (not easy / not impossible)" | Didn't research actual capex / talent / regulatory barriers | Expansion strategy unrealistic |
| **Industry maturity** | "Market is mature (not growth / declining)" | Based on revenue trends only; no adoption curve analysis | Growth strategy inappropriate for mature market |
| **Decision-maker incentives** | "Buyers are rational actors (not subject to bias / budget cycles / internal politics)" | Assumed economic logic; ignored organizational reality | Strategy doesn't account for procurement process |

**Common market assumption errors:**
- Assuming a competitive market without analyzing concentration
- Projecting your own priorities onto customers
- Treating "possible" as "likely" (anyone *could* enter; few actually
  do)
- Confusing current market state with permanent structure
- Assuming decision-makers follow logic rather than organizational
  incentives

---

### 5. Interpretation Assumptions
**"What we didn't find means what we think it means"**

Interpretation assumptions govern what **silence, absence, or lack of
data** tells us.

| Assumption | Example | Red Flag | Impact if Wrong |
|-----------|---------|----------|-----------------|
| **Silence = low priority** | "Subject doesn't mention Topic Y in public statements, so it's not a priority" | Subject may avoid topic for strategic reasons; topic may be sensitive | Risk assessment misses internal priority |
| **Absence = non-existence** | "We found no reports of incident X, so it didn't happen" | Incidents unreported, underreported, or suppressed | Actual risk is higher than research suggests |
| **Lack of data = lack of activity** | "No hiring announcements = subject isn't hiring" | Data sources lag; announcements are optional | Competitive threat underestimated |
| **One example = pattern** | "One startup in the space shows growth, so the market is growing" | Survivor bias; other startups failed silently | Market size overestimated |
| **Negative sentiment = market rejection** | "Critics are vocal about the product, so it's failing" | Critics disproportionately vocal; satisfied users silent | Adoption underestimated |

**Common interpretation errors:**
- Assuming absence of evidence = evidence of absence
- Treating surface-level silence as proof of irrelevance
- Counting observed signals without accounting for observation bias
- Generalizing from one example without sample size check
- Forgetting that data sources have gaps, not that reality has them

## Assumption Identification Checklist

Use this checklist **during VERIFY phase** when scoring sources. For
each key claim, run through:

### For Every Key Claim, Ask:

☐ **What is the claim?**
→ State it in one sentence

☐ **What must be true for this claim to be valid?**
→ List all preconditions

☐ **Did we verify each precondition directly?**
→ Check against source log; did we test this or assume it?

☐ **Which preconditions did we NOT verify?**
→ These are assumptions

☐ **For each assumption: if it's wrong, how does the report change?**
→ Rate impact: High / Medium / Low

☐ **Does this assumption change our confidence level in the claim?**
→ If a High-impact assumption is unverified, downgrade claim confidence

## Impact Assessment: Rating Assumption Severity

Not all assumptions matter equally. Rate each by **impact on findings**,
not by how likely it is to be wrong.

### **HIGH IMPACT** — Report conclusion flips if assumption is wrong

| Assumption | Scenario | Consequence |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| Source is truthful | Company A lies about hiring → reveals nothing about market | All claims from A become suspect; competitor threat assessment wrong |
| Causality is correct | Hiring driven by panic, not confidence → signals distress, not strength | Strategic recommendation flips |
| Scope applies | Pattern is local-only, not regional — local phenomenon, not trend | Expansion recommendation works in one market, fails in another |
| Market assumption holds | Market is duopoly, not competitive → different strategy set applies | "Differentiate" fails; must "cooperate" instead |

**→ Any High-impact assumption that is unverified must be disclosed in
the report. Consider downgrading claim confidence.**

### **MEDIUM IMPACT** — Report findings remain directionally correct but
magnitude changes

| Assumption | Scenario | Consequence |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| Source completeness | Data source captures 60% of hires, not 80% | Actual hiring is higher than data shows; order of magnitude correct |
| Causality magnitude | A regulatory driver causes 70% of hiring, not 100% | Other drivers are significant; not the only lever |
| Segment scope | Insight applies to mid-market + enterprise, not smaller firms | Recommendation works but requires segmentation |
| Interpretation | Silence means lower priority, not absent priority | Topic is secondary, not irrelevant |

**→ Disclose in assumptions section. Note that findings hold
directionally but may understate/overstate magnitude.**

### **LOW IMPACT** — Report is robust even if assumption is wrong

| Assumption | Scenario | Consequence |
|-----------|----------|-------------|
| Source expertise recency | Analyst last worked in field years ago | Trend analysis still valid; current tactics may be outdated |
| Geographic scope | Pattern holds for a cluster of markets but not the whole region | Recommendation works for target market even if not universal |
| Market maturity classification | Market is late-growth, not mature | Growth strategies still applicable; timing varies |

**→ Can be mentioned briefly or noted in background; not material to
conclusion.**

## Assumption Exposure Matrix

Track all assumptions for each key claim using this template:

| Key Claim | Category | Assumption | Verified? | If Wrong... | Impact | Disclose? |
|-----------|----------|-----------|-----------|----------|--------|-----------|
| Subject is hiring compliance staff | SOURCE | Job board is current | No | Numbers are stale | MEDIUM | Yes |
| Subject is hiring compliance staff | CAUSALITY | Hiring = confidence (not panic) | No | Threat = opposite direction | HIGH | Yes |
| Hiring is a regional trend | SCOPE | Pattern applies region-wide | No | Pattern is one-market-only | HIGH | Yes |
| Market rewards compliance prep | MARKET | Buyers prioritize compliance | No | Other factors dominate decision | MEDIUM | Yes |
| Rising compliance spend | INTERPRETATION | Silence = low priority was wrong | Yes (evidence gathered) | Risk underestimated | LOW | Brief note |

## Disclosure Template: How to Present Assumptions in Report

**Location:** Assumptions section goes in the report's **Assumptions
and Limitations** block.

**Format:** Numbered list (same order as key claims appear in report).
For each:
1. State the assumption clearly
2. Explain why we made it
3. State the impact if it's wrong
4. Note whether we partially verified it

### Example: Disclosure in Research Report

```
ASSUMPTIONS THAT COULD CHANGE THESE FINDINGS

This report rests on the following material assumptions. If any of these
proves incorrect, the findings and recommendations should be
re-evaluated.

1. Job Board Currency
   Assumption: The subject's job board reflects current, active
   positions (not old postings, not hiring forecasts)
   Why: Job boards are self-reported; HR teams don't always remove
   closed positions
   Impact (if wrong): Actual headcount change is 20-40% lower than data
   suggests
   Verification: Spot-checked 5 postings; all verified as current.
   Partial confidence.
   Confidence adjustment: Downgrade hiring magnitude from CONFIRMED to
   LIKELY

2. Causality Direction
   Assumption: Increased compliance hiring reflects market confidence,
   not distressed hiring (acquisition prep, mandatory headcount, crisis
   response)
   Why: Multiple motives possible; we inferred from public signals
   Impact (if wrong): Competitive threat assessment reverses (weakness
   masked as strength)
   Verification: None direct. Supported by industry trend reporting.
   Confidence adjustment: Downgrade causality claim from CONFIRMED to
   ESTIMATED
   Recommendation: If acting on this, verify motive through direct
   inquiry

3. Regional Applicability
   Assumption: Hiring patterns in one market reflect a broader regional
   trend (not a local regulatory anomaly)
   Why: No market-by-market hiring data collected
   Impact (if wrong): Recommendation works in one market, fails
   elsewhere
   Verification: Checked regulatory harmonization timeline. Partial
   confidence.
   Confidence adjustment: Note this as "high confidence in the source
   market, estimated confidence regionally"
   Recommendation: Validate market-specific hiring before extending the
   recommendation

4. Buyer Behavior (Market Assumption)
   Assumption: Buyers prioritize regulatory preparedness over cost
   Why: Regulatory environment suggests necessity, but business
   pressures suggest cost sensitivity
   Impact (if wrong): Price, not readiness, drives purchasing
   Verification: No direct buyer research conducted
   Confidence adjustment: Downgrade market insight from LIKELY to
   ESTIMATED
   Recommendation: Recommend buyer research before finalizing
   go-to-market strategy
```

## Common Assumption Mistakes

| Mistake | Example | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| **Listing obvious assumptions only** | "We assume sources exist," "We assume the internet works" | List material assumptions that affect findings, not mechanical preconditions |
| **Not assessing impact** | "We assume X is true. We assume Y is true." → No consequence stated | For each assumption, state: "If wrong, then..." |
| **Treating assumptions as failures** | Buried in appendix, defensive tone | Present matter-of-factly. Transparency builds trust. |
| **Too many assumptions** | A 47-item list of every possible precondition | List only material assumptions (would change conclusion if wrong). Aim for 5-12 per report. |
| **Assumptions not tied to claims** | Floating list disconnected from findings | For each key claim, name its assumptions. Reader can see what's at stake. |
| **Hiding uncertainties in prose** | "Research suggests that findings likely indicate..." | Make assumptions explicit. Use the four confidence labels (CONFIRMED/LIKELY/ESTIMATED/UNKNOWN). |
| **Over-confidence despite assumptions** | Assumptions listed but then ignored in conclusion | Adjust confidence language based on assumption verification. |

## Field Use: Assumption Workflow

### During VERIFY Phase (Source Scoring)

1. **List key claims** from your research (the 5-7 assertions your
   report depends on)
2. **For each claim, run the Assumption ID Checklist** (what must be
   true for this to hold?)
3. **Mark preconditions as VERIFIED or ASSUMPTION** (did you test it or
   assume it?)
4. **Rate impact** for each unverified assumption (High / Medium / Low)
5. **Document in the Assumption Exposure Matrix** (one row per
   assumption)
6. **Adjust claim confidence** based on assumption verification
   (CONFIRMED → LIKELY if a high-impact assumption is unverified)

### During REPORT Phase (Writing Limitations)

1. **Extract HIGH-impact assumptions** from the matrix (these go in the
   report)
2. **Extract MEDIUM-impact assumptions** that your strategy depends on
   (these go in the report)
3. **LOW-impact assumptions** can be omitted or noted briefly
4. **Write disclosure** using the template above (one paragraph per
   assumption)
5. **Tie each assumption back to its claim** (show the reader where it
   matters)
6. **Adjust confidence labels** in the finding based on assumption
   verification level
7. **Add an implementation note** if downstream action depends on the
   assumption holding

## Verification Gates

### Assumption Exposure Ready to Publish When:

✓ All HIGH-impact assumptions are listed and disclosed
✓ For each assumption, the impact statement answers "If wrong, then..."
✓ Confidence levels reflect assumption verification
✓ No unverified HIGH-impact assumption is treated as fact in conclusion
✓ Reader can see which claims are solid and which rest on assumptions

### Red Flags: Do Not Publish

❌ Assumptions not mentioned anywhere (hidden)
❌ HIGH-impact assumptions presented as certain
❌ Confidence language overstates what assumptions support
❌ Assumptions not tied to specific claims (orphan list)
❌ More than 3 unverified HIGH-impact assumptions (report unreliable)

## Sample Assumption Logs

### Scenario 1: Competitive Hiring Analysis

**Report Question:** Is a competitor building a new business line?
**Key Claim:** "The competitor's hiring pattern suggests new market
entry"

| Assumption | Why | Verified? | Impact | Disclose? |
|-----------|-----|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Hiring dept structure = business unit | Org structure reflects strategy | Partial (checked org chart) | MEDIUM | Yes, briefly |
| New hires aren't replacement | Growth headcount, not churn-driven | No (churn data unavailable) | HIGH | Yes, prominently |
| Hiring timeline indicates launch readiness | Typical lead time for market entry | Assumed (no launch date published) | MEDIUM | Yes |
| Market can absorb entrant | Won't collapse existing market | No (saturation analysis absent) | MEDIUM | Yes |

**Result:** Report states "Likely entering market (confidence ESTIMATED
due to unverified hiring intent and market assumptions)"

---

### Scenario 2: Regulatory Impact Analysis

**Report Question:** How will a new regulation affect industry hiring?
**Key Claim:** "The regulation will create a 30% compliance headcount
increase"

| Assumption | Why | Verified? | Impact | Disclose? |
|-----------|-----|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Regulation requires compliance staff (not just systems) | Regulations sometimes solvable with software | Partial (checked reg text; systems can't meet all requirements) | HIGH | Yes |
| Compliance staff complement is a small % of headcount | Based on historical precedent | Partial (checked several firm examples; a range) | HIGH | Yes |
| Timeline: hiring begins next quarter (not sooner, not delayed) | Effective date implies a prep lag | Assumed (no survey of firm timelines) | MEDIUM | Yes |
| No automation substitution for staff | Assumes human-based compliance, not tech | No (automation trends not analyzed) | HIGH | Yes |
| Scope: applies only to larger firms | Regulation exempts micro-enterprises | Verified (regulation text checked) | — | No (verified) |

**Result:** Report estimates a range (ESTIMATED, due to compensation
model uncertainty, automation substitution, and timeline variation by
firm). High-risk assumptions: baseline % of workforce, automation
displacement.

---

### Scenario 3: Market Trend Analysis

**Report Question:** Is the market shifting toward a new service
model?
**Key Claim:** "Top firms are adopting the new model as preferred
approach"

| Assumption | Why | Verified? | Impact | Disclose? |
|-----------|-----|-----------|--------|-----------|
| "Top firms" = top firms by a defined criterion | Could mean different tiers depending on definition | Specified in methodology; verified | — | No (defined) |
| Public statements reflect actual strategy (not messaging) | Companies may state strategy while executing differently | No (no private behavior data) | HIGH | Yes |
| Adoption = genuine preference (not forced by circumstance) | May adopt because it's cheaper, not better | No (motive analysis not conducted) | MEDIUM | Yes |
| Trend continues (not cyclical reversal) | May be temporary response to current conditions | No (multi-year projection made without cyclicality analysis) | MEDIUM | Yes |
| Definition of the model is stable across firms | Firms may call the same thing different names | Partial (checked a sample; fairly consistent) | LOW | Briefly note |

**Result:** Report states "Leading firms adopting the model (LIKELY based
on public positioning, but actual deployment motive and sustainability
unverified. Recommend private interviews to validate preference vs.
circumstantial adoption.)"

## Quick Reference: Assumption Taxonomy

**Keep this handy during research:**

**SOURCE ASSUMPTIONS:** Is the source truthful, complete, current,
independent, expert?
**CAUSALITY ASSUMPTIONS:** Does X cause Y (or does Y cause X, or does Z
cause both)?
**SCOPE ASSUMPTIONS:** Does this finding apply here, in this segment, at
this time, to this use?
**MARKET ASSUMPTIONS:** Is the market structured as we assume
(competitive, rational, mature)?
**INTERPRETATION ASSUMPTIONS:** Does silence mean what we think? Does
absence mean non-existence?

---

**Field use:** Assumption Exposure happens in VERIFY (document), then in
REPORT (disclose). A well-exposed report builds trust even when findings
are uncertain. A report hiding assumptions loses credibility the moment
they're discovered.
