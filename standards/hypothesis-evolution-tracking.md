# Hypothesis Evolution Tracking

## Introduction

Hypothesis evolution is where research proves itself intellectually
honest. At the heart of this method is a commitment to truth-seeking,
which means acknowledging what you expected to find, documenting what
you actually found, and having the rigor to admit when the evidence
changes your mind. This framework prevents confirmation bias — the most
dangerous research error — and creates an audit trail that lets you,
your team, and your readers see exactly how you reasoned through the
evidence and where your thinking shifted.

Every research run begins with a hypothesis (BRIEF phase). You form an
expectation based on background knowledge, initial signals, or the
research question itself. That hypothesis isn't wrong — it's your
starting point. But research exists to test that starting point against
reality, and if reality contradicts you, that's not failure. That's the
whole purpose of the work. Hypothesis Evolution Tracking documents this
process explicitly: what you believed before research, what the sources
actually said during research, and what your honest conclusion is after
evaluating all evidence. This creates three documentary layers that
reveal not just your final answer but your reasoning path.

The real value is methodological rigor. When you force yourself to
document "Expected → Observed → Actual," you catch yourself mid-slide
into confirmation bias. You can't accidentally hide the fact that three
sources contradicted your initial hypothesis because you wrote it down.
You can't claim "nuance" when the data actually refuted you because the
comparison is right there. And you can't let a reader second-guess your
methodology because the reasoning is explicit and checkable. This is how
research becomes trustworthy — not through perfect initial guesses, but
through transparent documentation of where the evidence led you.

Hypothesis Evolution Tracking lives at the intersection of research
rigor and intellectual honesty. It transforms research from "find
information that supports what you already believe" into "find what's
true and adjust your understanding accordingly."

### How This System Works: The Three-State Model

Hypothesis Evolution Tracking has three components that work
sequentially across the research cycle:

**1. Expected (Pre-Research Hypothesis)** — Before you gather evidence,
state explicitly what you think you'll find. What's your working
hypothesis? What does existing knowledge suggest? This becomes your
baseline.

**2. Observed (During-Research Findings)** — As you gather sources and
evidence, document what they actually say. Where do observations match
expectations? Where do they diverge? What surprised you? This is the raw
material — no interpretation yet, just "what did the sources report?"

**3. Actual (Post-Research Conclusion)** — After evaluating all evidence
through the lens of source quality, triangulation, and verification,
state your honest final conclusion. Did evidence CONFIRM the hypothesis,
REFUTE it, or COMPLICATE it? How did your understanding shift? This is
where reasoning becomes visible.

The power is in the contrast. By documenting all three states, you
create an observable reasoning path that can be audited, tested, and
explained. A reader can see you didn't cherry-pick sources. Your team can
verify that you updated your views based on evidence. And future research
can learn from where evidence surprised you, since those surprise points
often reveal important blind spots or underlying assumptions in the
field.

This system also prevents a dangerous trap in research: the false claim
of "nuance." Nuance is real and valuable when evidence is genuinely
mixed — when 40% of sources point one direction and 60% point another and
you describe both. But when 90% of your sources contradict your initial
hypothesis and you call that "nuance," you're hiding from the evidence.
Hypothesis Evolution Tracking won't let you do that, because the Actual
state forces you to name the outcome clearly: CONFIRMED / REFUTED /
COMPLICATED.

## Why Three States, Not Two?

You might ask: why not just track "what I thought" versus "what I
found"? The answer is that "what I found" and "what I concluded" are
different things, and collapsing them destroys precision.

**Observed** captures raw source material: what did reporters, analysts,
companies, and experts actually state? This is fact-level. The sources
said X. Multiple sources said Y. One anomalous source said Z.

**Actual** is your synthesis: weighing those sources using
`source-scoring.md`, triangulating across perspectives, and resolving
conflicts to reach a reasoned conclusion. You might observe that three
sources say "Company is expanding" and one says "Company is
contracting," and your actual conclusion is "Company is selectively
expanding in high-margin sectors while contracting elsewhere," which is
a synthesis that requires source evaluation and judgment.

By maintaining three states, you document both the raw evidence
(Observed) and your analytical reasoning (Actual). This allows a reader
to verify your source work independently of your conclusion — they can
agree or disagree with your sources, check your scoring logic, and reach
their own synthesis. Transparency at the evidence layer is what builds
trust.

## The Three States

### STATE 1: EXPECTED (Pre-Research Hypothesis)

**The Purpose: Documenting Your Baseline**

Before you gather sources, you operate from existing knowledge,
background signals, or the research question itself. You form a working
hypothesis. This state documents what you expected to find and why. It
serves three functions:

1. **Creates an accountability anchor** — You can't accidentally rewrite
   history about what you believed before if you wrote it down
2. **Surfaces underlying assumptions** — When you state your hypothesis
   explicitly, you discover what you're taking for granted
3. **Enables honest evaluation later** — You'll use this as the
   reference point when you assess whether evidence confirmed, refuted,
   or complicated your thinking

**What to Document in Expected**

- **What is my working hypothesis?** State it in one clear sentence.
- **What is my evidence for this hypothesis?** Name your starting
  signals.
- **What would confirmation look like?** Describe what sources would
  need to say for you to feel confident the hypothesis is correct.
- **What would refutation look like?** Describe signals that would
  contradict the hypothesis.
- **What am I assuming about the world?** Make your underlying
  assumptions visible.

**Why These Questions Matter**

The first three questions (hypothesis, evidence, confirmation) are
obvious. The fourth (refutation) is crucial because it forces you to
consider what would prove you wrong. Researchers naturally look for
confirmation signals and miss disconfirmation signals unless explicitly
asked. The fifth (assumptions) is harder but more valuable because it
reveals where your thinking might be blind.

**Language and Framing for Expected**

Use confident but provisional language. "Based on initial signals, I
hypothesize that..." This isn't wishy-washy; it's honest. You're not
claiming certainty; you're stating a starting point that research will
test.

Avoid overconfidence: don't frame the hypothesis as already proven. Also
avoid passive language that obscures your thinking. Taking ownership of
the hypothesis makes the later comparison meaningful.

**Common Mistakes in Expected**

- **Hedging so much the hypothesis disappears.** Commit to a specific
  hypothesis.
- **Making the hypothesis too broad.** Make it specific enough that
  evidence can actually test it.
- **Confusing hopes with hypotheses.**
- **Stating the hypothesis but skipping the reasoning.** Always explain
  why you hold this hypothesis. That reasoning is where blind spots
  hide.
- **Using circular evidence.** Find independent signals that motivated
  the hypothesis.

### STATE 2: OBSERVED (During-Research Findings)

**The Purpose: Documenting Raw Evidence**

As you gather and score sources, this section documents what those
sources actually say. This is the evidence layer — uninterpreted but
organized. You're not yet concluding whether the evidence confirms or
refutes your hypothesis. You're documenting what the primary and
secondary sources reported.

Observed differs critically from a simple literature review: you're not
summarizing everything you found, you're documenting what you found in
relation to your hypothesis.

**What to Document in Observed**

1. **Evidence that supports the hypothesis** — Name the sources, include
   their scores, describe what they claimed.
2. **Evidence that contradicts the hypothesis** — Which sources, what
   claims, how credible are they?
3. **Evidence that complicates the hypothesis** — Sources that partially
   support, add nuance, introduce conditions, or reveal complexity?
4. **Evidence on underlying assumptions** — Did sources validate or
   challenge the assumptions you documented in Expected?
5. **Gaps and anomalies** — What did you expect to find but didn't?
   What surprised you? What sources contradicted each other?

**Important: Source Quality Notation**

Note source band alongside each finding — GOLD / SILVER / BRONZE /
QUESTIONABLE — even at this raw-observation stage, so quality context
travels with the evidence into synthesis.

**Language and Framing for Observed**

Use reporting language, not conclusion language. Say "Sources reported
that..." not "This proves..." Be precise about source characteristics
and frequency ("Four out of five sources mentioned..." vs. "One isolated
source mentioned...").

**Common Mistakes in Observed**

- **Editorializing instead of reporting.**
- **Losing source context.**
- **Oversimplifying conflicting sources** — don't average contradictory
  sources into a false middle. Document the disagreement explicitly.
- **Cherry-picking observed evidence** — you're supposed to document ALL
  significant findings, not just the ones that support your hypothesis.
- **Confusing "sources mentioned topic X" with "topic X is true."**

### STATE 3: ACTUAL (Post-Research Conclusion)

**The Purpose: Stating Your Reasoned Final Conclusion**

After evaluating all observed evidence through source quality,
triangulation, and verification, you state your honest final conclusion.
This is where your synthesis becomes visible. You name clearly whether
the evidence CONFIRMED, REFUTED, or COMPLICATED your hypothesis and
explain how your understanding shifted.

**What to Document in Actual**

1. **Direct answer to the hypothesis:** Did evidence CONFIRM, REFUTE, or
   COMPLICATE it? Be clear and direct.
2. **Why this conclusion?** Which sources were most credible? Which
   evidence carried the most weight? Where did different sources
   contradict and how did you resolve that?
3. **How did your understanding shift?** Name how your thinking changed
   from Expected to Actual.
4. **Confidence level:** How confident are you in this conclusion? Where
   are the gaps in evidence?
5. **Caveats and limitations:** What's uncertain? What could change this
   conclusion?

**Confirming vs. Refuting vs. Complicating**

These three terms have distinct meanings and you must choose the right
one:

- **CONFIRMED:** The preponderance of evidence aligns with your
  hypothesis. Multiple high-quality sources support it. Contradicting
  evidence is minimal or low-quality.
- **REFUTED:** The preponderance of evidence contradicts your
  hypothesis. High-quality sources show your hypothesis was wrong. You
  must update your understanding.
- **COMPLICATED:** Evidence is genuinely mixed. Your hypothesis was
  partially correct but incomplete. Reality is more nuanced than your
  original framing.

The critical difference: CONFIRMED and REFUTED are oppositional
conclusions. COMPLICATED means your hypothesis was pointing in the right
direction but was incomplete or oversimplified.

**Note:** Avoid the false refuge of "nuance" when evidence actually
refutes you. If 85% of evidence contradicts your hypothesis and 15%
supports it, you don't get to call that "nuanced." That's refutation.
Nuance is when evidence genuinely is mixed (50-50 or 40-60 splits).

**Language and Framing for Actual**

Use clear declarative language. Take a stand. Also own the reasoning:
explain why you weighted one source over another. This makes your
reasoning auditable.

Avoid false certainty: a confirmed hypothesis isn't "proven beyond
doubt." This is rigor, not weakness.

**Common Mistakes in Actual**

- **Hedging so heavily the conclusion disappears.**
- **Changing the hypothesis instead of admitting refutation.**
- **Using "nuance" as a hiding place.**
- **Ignoring contradicting sources.**
- **Confusing "I didn't find evidence" with "evidence is mixed."** If
  you looked thoroughly and found no support for your hypothesis, that's
  not nuance, that's absence of evidence. Name it.
- **Letting reader preferences influence conclusion.**

## How Hypothesis Evolution Tracking Fits Into the Research Cycle

**Within PLAN (before INTEL):**
Before you enter INTEL, create the Expected section. This documents your
pre-research hypothesis and grounds the rest of research in explicit
assumptions. It takes 30–60 minutes but saves hours of later confusion
about why you believed what you believed.

**During INTEL:**
Maintain an ongoing Observed section as you gather sources. Update it
continuously as you score sources and document findings. Don't wait
until the end to try to remember what sources said. Real-time
documentation prevents cherry-picking and bias.

**At SYNTHESIZE and REPORT:**
Complete the Actual section as you finish source evaluation and begin
synthesis. Use it as the framework for your synthesis narrative and your
final report. The Actual section becomes the structure for how you
present findings.

**Integration with Source Scoring**

Hypothesis Evolution Tracking works alongside `source-scoring.md`:
- **Source Scoring** evaluates individual sources
- **Hypothesis Evolution Tracking** evaluates your overall reasoning
  (Expected → Observed → Actual)

Together, they create accountability at both the source level and the
synthesis level. You can't hide bias in individual sources (Source
Scoring exposes it) or in overall reasoning (Hypothesis Evolution
Tracking exposes it).

## Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

### Pitfall 1: Confirmation Bias Through Selective Observed Documentation
**What happens:** You document sources that support your hypothesis
carefully, but summarize or skip contradicting sources.
**How to prevent it:** Force yourself to give equal documentary
attention to contradicting evidence.
**Red flag:** If your Observed section reads like a narrative that
smoothly leads to your conclusion, you've probably hidden contradictions.

### Pitfall 2: Moving the Hypothesis to Match Evidence
**What happens:** Instead of admitting refutation, you subtly reframe
the hypothesis until it's so broad it can't be refuted.
**How to prevent it:** Lock your Expected hypothesis early. If you need
to update it based on evidence, document that explicitly.

### Pitfall 3: Calling Refutation "Nuance"
**What happens:** Evidence clearly contradicts your hypothesis, but you
characterize it as "nuanced" to avoid admitting you were wrong.
**How to prevent it:** Use this test: if you were betting money, would
you bet on your hypothesis or against it based on evidence? If against,
it's refutation, not nuance.

### Pitfall 4: Under-documenting Assumptions
**What happens:** You state a hypothesis but don't surface the
assumptions underneath it.
**How to prevent it:** Write the "Underlying assumptions" section in
Expected. Make every assumption visible.

### Pitfall 5: Letting Evidence Quality Disappear
**What happens:** You document Observed findings but lose track of
source quality.
**How to prevent it:** Use source quality tags consistently in
Observed. When you write Actual, explicitly reference source quality in
your reasoning.

### Pitfall 6: Forgetting That Observed and Actual Are Different
**What happens:** You collapse Observed and Actual into one section.
Your "conclusion" is just source aggregation, not analysis.
**How to prevent it:** Force separation. Observed is "what sources
said." Actual is "what I conclude after evaluating source quality."

## Assessment Rubric: Evaluating Your Own Hypothesis Evolution

Score yourself on these criteria after completing Expected → Observed →
Actual:

- **Hypothesis Clarity (0–5)** — Does Expected state the hypothesis
  clearly and specifically enough that Observed can actually test it?
- **Assumption Transparency (0–5)** — Did you surface your underlying
  assumptions? Are they reasonable?
- **Evidence Completeness (0–5)** — Does Observed document all
  significant findings, including contradictions?
- **Source Quality Notation (0–5)** — Is Observed clear about source
  quality, or is it mixing strong and weak sources without distinction?
- **Reasoning Transparency (0–5)** — Does Actual explain *why* you
  reached your conclusion?
- **Honest Outcome (0–5)** — Does Actual state clearly whether you
  CONFIRMED, REFUTED, or COMPLICATED the hypothesis, or do you hedge to
  avoid admitting refutation?
- **Confidence Calibration (0–5)** — Does Actual match your confidence
  to your evidence?

**Scoring:** 30+ points = sound and transparent reasoning. 20–30 = good
process with some gaps. 10–20 = significant gaps in transparency or
reasoning. <10 = major problems in research rigor.

## Worked Examples: Hypothesis Evolution in Practice

### Example 1: Hypothesis CONFIRMED

**Subject:** Klarna (fintech payments company)

**Expected Hypothesis:** Klarna's 2023 profitability pressure was driven
by aggressive international expansion into European markets that didn't
achieve expected unit economics.

**Observed:**
- [GOLD] Klarna's own financial disclosures show European operations
  losses growing sharply year over year while US operations stayed
  profitable.
- [SILVER] The CEO, on record, described European unit economics as
  "challenging."
- [SILVER] Multiple independent analyst reports each noted customer
  acquisition costs in mature European markets approaching or exceeding
  lifetime value.
- No credible source contradicted the pattern; one article noted
  macroeconomic headwinds as a compounding factor, not a rival
  explanation.

**Actual:**
*Outcome: CONFIRMED.* The preponderance of evidence — financial
disclosures, CEO statements, and independent analyst reports —
consistently points to European expansion as the primary driver of the
profitability pressure. Confidence: very high. No credible source
contradicted the hypothesis.

### Example 2: Hypothesis REFUTED

**Subject:** A major AI lab's business model strategy

**Expected Hypothesis:** The company is pursuing a high-volume consumer
product strategy as its primary business model, with enterprise/API as
secondary revenue.

**Observed:**
- [SILVER] Consumer product adoption was widely reported as
  fastest-growing in its category.
- [GOLD] The CEO, in a named interview, stated: "Enterprise and API are
  the core of our business model. That's where the revenue comes from.
  Consumer is distribution."
- [GOLD] Investor materials show enterprise revenue at the large
  majority of total, with API usage from named enterprise customers
  documented as the primary revenue source.
- [SILVER] Multiple analyst firms independently characterized the model
  as B2B-through-API, with the consumer tier as a loss-leader for brand
  building.

**Actual:**
*Outcome: REFUTED.* Evidence directly contradicts the hypothesis.
Multiple high-quality sources (a direct CEO statement, investor
financial data, independent analyst consensus) show enterprise/API
revenue dominates. The confusion was mistaking consumer *visibility* for
business-model *priority* — a scale/priority distinction the original
hypothesis conflated. Confidence: very high.

---

### Example 3: Hypothesis COMPLICATED

**Subject:** A major EV manufacturer's supply-chain strategy

**Expected Hypothesis:** The company is pursuing wholesale vertical
integration, building most components in-house to capture margin and
control.

**Observed:**
- [GOLD] Investor presentations document significant in-house
  investment in battery production and semiconductor design.
- [GOLD] Supply-chain analysis from multiple sources shows the majority
  of components are still sourced externally; one analyst calculated
  actual vertical integration at roughly a quarter of total supply need.
- [SILVER] Partnership agreements show increasing use of external
  semiconductor manufacturers for production, even as chip *design*
  stays in-house — a design/production split the simple hypothesis
  missed.

**Actual:**
*Outcome: COMPLICATED.* The hypothesis overstated the case. This is
*selective* vertical integration — deep in-house capability in the
components that are strategically critical (batteries, chip design), but
continued heavy reliance on external supply for commodity components.
The better frame is "what does the company vertically integrate, and
why?" rather than a binary yes/no. Confidence: high on the specific
sourcing split; medium on the underlying strategic rationale, since
internal cost/margin data isn't public.

## Field Use: When and How to Deploy This

**For research teams:** Use Expected–Observed–Actual as the core
documentation framework for every run. It catches bias early and creates
accountability. When teammates disagree about conclusions, the framework
forces explicit discussion of evidence quality and assumptions.

**For report delivery:** Include a compressed version of Hypothesis
Evolution Tracking in the Report as the "how we reasoned" note. Readers
appreciate transparency about how you evaluated evidence and changed
your mind — it builds trust because they can see you're not
cherry-picking.

**For audit and verification:** This creates an audit trail. If a
finding is challenged months later, you can point to the Observed
section and show exactly which sources supported which claims, weighted
by quality, and that you considered contradicting evidence.

**For learning and improvement:** At the end of a research cycle, review
your Expected → Actual shift. Did you systematically mispredict
something? Use these patterns to improve future hypotheses.

## Conclusion: The Discipline of Changed Minds

Hypothesis Evolution Tracking enforces a simple discipline: you must
document what you believed before, what you discovered during, and what
you actually concluded after. This leaves no room for unconscious
revision or selective reporting.

The real power isn't in the documentation itself — it's in the
intellectual honesty that documentation requires. When you know you'll
have to explain why evidence led you to a different conclusion than you
expected, you take evidence seriously. When you know you'll have to name
whether evidence confirmed, refuted, or complicated your hypothesis, you
can't pretend nuance is certainty.

Research is supposed to move you toward truth. Hypothesis Evolution
Tracking ensures it actually does.
