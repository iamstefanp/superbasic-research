# Decay Classes

**Used at:** SCOPE (Phase 2) — set the recency gate.

## How to use this

"How old can a source be?" has no single answer, and any research process that picks one — "nothing older than six months" — will be wrong in both directions at once: too permissive on a regulation that changed last week, absurdly strict on an institution's founding values. Age is not the variable. The variable is **how fast the subject itself changes**, and a source is only as stale as the world it describes.

So at SCOPE you do one thing: identify what your subject actually is, assign it a decay class, and **state the resulting cutoff in the Scope document** — the ideal window, the acceptable limit, and the age past which a source is a red flag rather than a source. That cutoff is then binding for the rest of the run. It governs what INTEL goes looking for, and it is the ladder that VERIFY scores the Recency dimension against. Setting it late, or leaving it implicit, is how a run ends up with a well-sourced answer to the wrong year's question.

Most runs have one dominant subject and take one class. Runs that genuinely span tempos are handled under **Mixed-tempo subjects** below — but resist splitting by default, because a scope with three decay classes is usually three scopes wearing one coat.

## The classes

| Class | Half-life | Subject types | Recency gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **FAST** | ~3 months | Regulatory and legal change; compliance mandates; pricing and product launches; hiring and open roles; quarterly financial results; crisis and emergency response; live market movement; anything with an active news cycle or a legislative calendar attached | **Ideal 0–3 months · Acceptable to 6 months · Red flag beyond 12 months.** Score 5 within 30 days, 4 at 1–3 months, 3 at 3–6 months, 2 at 6–12 months, 1 beyond. |
| **MEDIUM** | ~18 months | Operating structure and systems; technology adoption; partnerships and alliances; executive and org charts; team composition; market trends; competitive positioning; sector benchmarks; programme design | **Ideal 0–6 months · Acceptable to 12 months · Red flag beyond 24 months.** Score 5 within 6 months, 4 at 6–12 months, 3 at 1–2 years, 2 at 2–3 years, 1 beyond. |
| **SLOW** | ~5 years | Strategy and long-term positioning; market structure; institutional culture and values; brand identity; professional norms; demographic composition; foundational academic research in a settled field | **Ideal 0–2 years · Acceptable to 5 years · Red flag beyond 10 years.** Score 5 within 12 months, 4 at 1–2 years, 3 at 2–3 years, 2 at 3–5 years, 1 beyond. |
| **PERMANENT** | none | Settled historical fact; founding events and dates; the text of a published work; a completed election result; the terms of an executed and expired agreement; physical constants and closed scientific questions; anything that happened and cannot un-happen | **No cutoff — age is not a defect.** Score 5 regardless of date, provided the source is otherwise sound. A contemporaneous account of a past event usually *outscores* a recent one on Proximity, so an old source here is frequently the better source. |

*(FAST / MEDIUM / SLOW and their gates come from the existing standard. PERMANENT and the half-life column are new in this version — see the note at the end.)*

Half-life is a working intuition, not arithmetic: it is roughly the interval over which half of what a source asserts about that subject stops being reliably true. Use it to sanity-check a class assignment. If you would not bet that half of a document's claims were still accurate after the stated interval, you have assigned the class too slow.

## Judging a subject's decay class

Take the research question as written and ask, in order:

**1. What kind of thing does this question describe — an event, a state, or a nature?**
Events resolve and stop changing, and tend toward PERMANENT once concluded. States are how something currently stands, and decay at the rate the thing itself moves — FAST or MEDIUM. Natures are what something fundamentally is, and move slowly if at all — SLOW.

**2. What would have to happen for the answer to change, and how often does that happen?**
This is the decisive test. A rule changes when a legislature or regulator acts — which happens on a calendar measured in months, so FAST. An organisation's systems change when it runs a procurement and a migration — eighteen months to two years, so MEDIUM. An institution's culture changes when its people and its incentives change, which takes a generation of staff — SLOW. Name the mechanism of change, estimate its frequency, and the class falls out.

**3. Is there a clock or a calendar attached?**
Statutory deadlines, reporting cycles, election dates, funding rounds, seasonal patterns, product cadences — a subject with an external calendar is at least MEDIUM and usually FAST, because the calendar guarantees change whether or not anything else prompts it.

**4. Has there been a break?**
A discontinuity — a change of government, a regulation taking effect, a pandemic, a technology that resets the baseline, a merger, a leadership change — invalidates material from before it regardless of class. Even a SLOW subject has a hard floor at the break. When a break exists inside your window, say so in the Scope document and set the cutoff at the break, not at the class default. This is the single most common way a correctly-classed scope still produces a wrong answer.

**5. If it is genuinely borderline, class it faster.**
The cost of an over-strict gate is extra searching. The cost of an over-loose gate is a confident finding about a world that no longer exists. These are not symmetrical.

### Mixed-tempo subjects

When a run genuinely spans tempos — a study of an organisation's regulatory exposure *and* its culture — do not average the classes into a middle that fits neither. Score recency **per field**, and weight each by its importance to the conclusion:

> Regulatory landscape (FAST): 85% of sources within 3 months → 5/5 × 40% = 2.0
> Sector benchmarks (MEDIUM): 70% within 6 months → 4/5 × 30% = 1.2
> Institutional culture (SLOW): 60% within 2 years → 3/5 × 30% = 0.9
> **Weighted recency: 4.1/5**

State the split and the weights in the Scope document. If you cannot state the weights, the scope is not decided yet.

### Historical context exception

Older sources are legitimate, whatever the class, when they are used **explicitly for historical context or to establish a trend over time** — clearly flagged as background rather than current state, and supplemented by recent sources for anything present-tense. A study of an institution's continuity that draws on fifteen years of material is doing exactly the right thing, *provided* recent sources confirm the pattern still holds today. The exception covers using an old source as history. It never covers using an old source as news.

## Common mistakes

**One timeframe for every subject** — "all sources under six months," applied regardless of topic. Match the window to the subject's actual rate of change.

**Treating old sources as current** — citing figures from four years ago for this year's landscape without saying so. Flag old material as historical context, in the text, where the reader meets it.

**Ignoring subject tempo** — using eighteen-month-old material on an implementation that has run through two full cycles since. Some subjects change daily, some annually, some generationally, and the same date means three different things across them.

**Counting sources equally** — 30% recent plus 70% old is not "we have recent sources." Calculate the actual percentage within the window and score that number.

**Classing by domain rather than by question** — "this is a legal topic, therefore FAST." The text of a statute passed in 1974 and never amended is PERMANENT; next month's compliance deadline under it is FAST. The question decides, not the field.

---

## Field use

Identify the subject → assign the class → state the cutoff in the Scope document → calculate the percentage of sources inside the window → flag any source used outside it for a current-state claim.
