# Source Personas

**Used at:** INTEL (Phase 4) — tag every source. CHECK (Phase 5) — test pool diversity.

## How to use this

Every source you collect gets exactly one persona tag, written next to it in the source pool — the persona is not a quality score, it is a statement of *what kind of place this came from and therefore what it can and cannot tell you*. Score tells you a source's quality; persona tells you its purpose. At CHECK, the pool must contain **at least two distinct personas**; a pool of one persona is not a research pool, it is a pool gathered from one kind of place, and it will reproduce that place's blind spots no matter how high the individual scores are. When a source could plausibly take two tags, tag it by who created it and why — creator and intent decide, not subject matter or tone.

**Status note (read this before tagging):** the persona set is **twelve**, all defined below, all equal. **Direct Witness** and **Regulatory Authority** were formalised after the original ten and count toward the two-persona diversity gate like any other. **Secondary Interpreter** is retired — see *Retired tags* below for the remapping.

## The personas — quick table

| Persona | One-line identity | What it is good for | Where it is weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Primary Artifact** | The subject speaking in its own voice — created *by* them, not *about* them | Verifiable specifics, revealed priorities, timeline of stated positions, self-conception | Best-foot-forward by construction; aspiration ≠ reality; no competitive or external context |
| **2. Independent Observer** | Professional truth-seeker with editorial standards and reputation at stake | Triangulated external perspective, professional verification, industry context, early pattern detection | One step removed; deadline-shallow; access-journalism softening; covers the newsworthy, not the normal |
| **3. Promotional Insider** | Real expertise deployed openly in service of commercial interests | Solutions landscape, genuine technical depth, industry vocabulary and mental models | Problems defined to fit their solution; competitor dismissal; urgency inflation; cherry-picked successes |
| **4. Captured Expert** | Sophisticated analysis whose funding or mission shapes every conclusion | Aggregated data nobody else can collect, benchmarks, cross-organisation frameworks | Structural capture; respondent self-selection; the damaging question is never asked |
| **5. Leaked Document** | High-value, high-risk: possibly hidden truth, possibly a trap | Hypothesis generation about the gap between public narrative and private reality | Unknown leaker motive; authenticity and completeness unverifiable; legal and ethical exposure |
| **6. Academic Authority** | Rigorous but slow — highest method standards, lowest timeliness | Theoretical frameworks, historical context, slow-moving cultural and structural questions | Years of publication lag; low proximity; samples often don't generalise to your subject |
| **7. Well-Meaning Generalist** | Honest but shallow — enthusiastic outsider synthesising conventional wisdom | Mainstream narrative, zeitgeist, how non-experts frame the issue, fresh-eyes naive questions | Confident errors; repeats marketing as fact; overgeneralises; propagates unverified statistics |
| **8. Anonymous Insider** | Claims insider knowledge, hides behind anonymity | Internal culture, employee experience, dysfunction no official source will state — **pattern only** | Motive unknowable; systematically skewed to extremes; single instances are worthless |
| **9. Tertiary Compiler** | Aggregates others' work without original observation | Rapid orientation, vocabulary, landscape map, pointers to real sources | Zero proximity; decay; selective inclusion; error propagation; AI fabrication |
| **10. Promotional Authority** | Expert turned salesperson — commercial capture wrapped in educational framing | Market positioning intelligence, dominant industry frameworks, vendor landscape maps | Claims independence it doesn't have; pay-to-play evaluation; self-serving urgency and market sizing |
| **11. Direct Witness** | A named person who was there, speaking for themselves about what they saw or lived | Unmediated specifics, sensory and sequential detail, the texture no summary carries, lived consequence | Reconstructive memory; one vantage point; no duty to context; invested in their own version; unfalsifiable both ways |
| **12. Regulatory Authority** | A body with statutory power stating a finding, requirement or enforcement action | On-the-record determinations with consequences attached, compelled disclosure, dates and thresholds you can build on | Slow; bound to its own remit; silent outside it; occasionally captured; absence of a finding proves nothing |

### Retired tags

| Tag | Status | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Secondary Interpreter** | **Retired.** It was never a distinct persona — it duplicated ground the set already covers. | Ask what the source actually does. If it **aggregates** other people's work and adds no original observation, tag **Tertiary Compiler**. If it **analyses** — a named person with a beat and a reputation building an argument from evidence they gathered — tag **Independent Observer**. ## Each persona in full

---

### 1. The Primary Artifact

**Character:** Direct evidence of action — what the subject actually did or said, unfiltered by any intermediary but inevitably shaped by what they choose to reveal.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 5 · Timeliness varies · Verifiability 4–5 · Independence 2–3 · Clarity 4–5 · Expertise 3–5 · **Typical total 23–27 (Gold/Silver Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

You encounter Primary Artifacts whenever the subject creates something themselves. The job posting on their careers page listing exact requirements for a role, seniority, and start date. The press release on their own site announcing a partnership. The executive's post sharing their view on regulation. The statutory filing showing revenue and headcount. The product launch describing new capabilities. A council's own published minutes. An artist's own liner notes.

These sources have an unmistakable quality: they speak in the subject's own voice, using their own language, framing their own narrative. No journalist interpretation, no analyst synthesis, no third-party filtering. **When you realise "this was created by them, not about them," you're looking at a Primary Artifact.**

The feeling is different. You're reading what they want you to know, presented how they want it understood. That directness is both the source's power and its limitation.

#### When to deploy

Primary Artifacts become essential when you're mapping aspirational identity and operational priorities.

**Understanding operational priorities.** You want to know whether organisations in a sector are genuinely prioritising something or just talking about it. Collect job postings from fifteen of them. Count: how many roles in that area, at what seniority, requiring what specific skills. A pattern of twelve of fifteen posting for it, eight at senior level, ten naming the specific challenge, tells you the priority is real — that is money and organisational attention, not marketing rhetoric.

What it does *not* reveal: whether the hires succeed (a posting is not a filled seat), whether the new people can actually solve the problem, whether this is reactive panic or strategic foresight, and whether the organisations posting *no* such roles are ahead (already prepared) or behind (ignoring it). Triangulate against Independent Observer reporting on readiness, Anonymous Insider signal on internal urgency, and Captured Expert survey data on preparation levels.

**Mapping stated strategy against operational reality.** An organisation's site claims it is "leading digital transformation" in its field. Interrogate that with more Primary Artifacts: what specific capabilities do they claim; are they hiring for those roles; what have they actually announced; how do leaders talk about it. A worked case:

- **Site claims:** AI-powered automation, cloud-based client portals, digital-first practice.
- **Job postings reality:** one digital transformation manager role, posted six months ago, still open; four traditional roles with no digital skills named; zero AI/ML roles.
- **Announcements:** one release about "exploring" AI, eighteen months ago, no follow-up.
- **Leadership posts:** partners posting about traditional values; no digital content in twelve months.

**Interpretation:** the transformation is aspirational marketing, not operational reality. The claims dramatically exceed the organisational commitment visible in hiring and leadership communication. *Primary Artifacts checked against other Primary Artifacts is a legitimate and powerful move.*

#### Characteristic strengths

**Verifiable specifics.** A job posting carries checkable facts: title, location, salary band, start date, required years and systems and languages. Every one is specific enough to verify or falsify. The role exists or it doesn't. The stated pay is what they claim or it isn't. The requirements are on the record, which creates accountability.

**Revealed priorities.** What an organisation chooses to hire for reveals what it actually needs, regardless of what it claims to be. If the site says "innovation leader" and every posting is for traditional roles, hiring reveals the truth.

**Timeline documentation.** Primary Artifacts leave a documentary trail. You can date when language changed, when priorities shifted, when initiatives appeared: first mention of a concept in site copy; first posting where it is "nice to have"; the posting where it becomes a requirement; the new site section devoted to it. That sequence shows evolution from awareness → consideration → urgency, with months attached.

**Self-conception.** How a subject describes itself — the language chosen, the values emphasised, the position claimed — reveals how it wants to be understood. That matters even when the self-conception doesn't match reality.

#### Characteristic weaknesses

**The best-foot-forward bias.** No organisation publishes Primary Artifacts that make it look bad. Releases announce successes, not failures. Postings describe ideal roles, not dysfunctional reality. Leadership posts share wins, not struggles. You will never see: "we're hiring a compliance chief because we've been non-compliant for years"; "this partnership announcement is a desperate attempt to appear innovative"; "our work-life balance culture is aspirational — we're still figuring it out." These realities may be true; Primary Artifacts systematically exclude them.

**Aspiration vs. reality gap.** What they say ≠ what they do. Site: "we prioritise employee development and continuous learning." Job posting from the same organisation: entry-level role, no mention of training or development. Anonymous Insider: recurring complaint that there is no professional development. The value is stated but not operationalised — the belief may be genuine while execution hasn't followed.

**Context requires external sources.** Primary Artifacts exist in isolation from competitive context, market reality, and external constraint. A posting seeking a scarce specialist doesn't tell you whether competitors are hiring the same role, whether that talent exists in that market at all, why they are hiring *now* (a deadline? client demand? competitor pressure?), or whether it is a new role or a backfill. You need Independent Observers and Captured Experts to supply the context that gives the artifact meaning.

#### Use cases in practice

**Operational signal detection.** Question: is a sector responding operationally to a mandate, or is the response purely rhetorical? Collect postings from twenty organisations over six months, announcements over twelve, tracked site changes, and leadership posts. Analysis: fifteen of twenty posted related roles; 87 postings naming the specific requirement in six months versus 12 in the previous six; salary bands rising (competitive hiring market); senior rather than junior roles. Announcements: twelve of twenty issued something; eight clustered in one quarter (urgency rising); language shifted from "preparing" to "ready to support clients." **Synthesis:** the response is real and accelerating; volume, seniority and pay signal genuine resource commitment. **Still needed:** Independent Observers on whether the mobilisation is *sufficient*; Captured Experts on what share are actually prepared versus merely hiring; Anonymous Insiders on whether internal reality matches the external hiring signal.

**Positioning analysis.** Question: how does this organisation position itself? Collect homepage and about text, service descriptions, case studies, leader bios, published thinking. Count positioning terms across all of it — "traditional/established/trusted" 23 mentions; "innovation/digital/modern" 4; "personal service/relationship" 18; "expertise/specialised" 31. Look at imagery: 90% offices, teams, traditional professional settings; 10% technology. **Synthesis:** the position is traditional expertise with personal service; digital is secondary or absent, and that is a deliberate choice. **Strategic read:** if the market is moving to digital-first providers, this is positioned as the alternative for buyers who value relationship — sustainable if that segment persists, vulnerable if it doesn't.

**When *not* to rely on Primary Artifacts.** Question: is the subject's product actually effective? Using only site claims and case studies is wrong, because the site will claim effectiveness regardless of reality, case studies are cherry-picked successes, and nobody publishes their failures. Correct approach: note what Primary Artifacts claim, then find Independent Observer coverage of real user experience, Anonymous Insider views on whether the thing works, and Captured Expert third-party evaluation or benchmarking. Only multi-persona triangulation assesses actual effectiveness.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **Unusually vague artifacts.** Postings should be specific. "Exciting opportunity in a dynamic environment! Looking for motivated professionals ready to make an impact!" — with no title, no experience requirement, no responsibilities, no pay, no start date — means one of three things: the role doesn't exist (posted to look active), the organisation doesn't know what it needs (dysfunction), or it is deliberately vague to cast a wide net (desperation). Treat with scepticism and look for the pattern across their other postings.
- **Stated values contradicted by operational artifacts.** Site: "we value work-life balance and wellbeing." Posting from the same organisation: "fast-paced environment, comfortable with evening and weekend work, high-pressure deadline culture." The gap matters more than either artifact alone.
- **Over-polished artifacts lacking authentic detail.** Real working documents usually carry small imperfections or informal language. Compare: "we're looking for someone to help us navigate this transition — honestly, we're figuring it out as we go and need expertise to help build our approach" against "join our world-class team at the forefront of excellence, leveraging cutting-edge solutions to deliver unparalleled value." The first admits reality. The second is rhetoric that may indicate there is nothing substantive behind it.

### 2. The Independent Observer

**Character:** Professional truth-seeker with editorial standards and reputation at stake, occupying the crucial middle ground between the subject's self-presentation and pure outside speculation.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 3–4 · Timeliness 4–5 · Verifiability 4–5 · Independence 4–5 · Clarity 4–5 · Expertise 3–4 · **Typical total 24–28 (Gold Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

Independent Observers are journalists and established reporters whose professional survival depends on credibility. You recognise them by editorial infrastructure.

- **Real byline:** a named reporter with a stated beat and a consistent history at the publication — not "Staff" or "Editorial Team."
- **Multiple perspectives:** the piece quotes the organisation, its customers or constituents, an analyst, and notes a competitor or opposing view.
- **Verification language:** "according to filings reviewed by this publication…"; "three executives interviewed for this story confirmed…"; "data from the industry association shows…"
- **Editorial hedging:** "the organisation claims… though independent verification was unavailable"; "while executives emphasised progress, users expressed concerns…"

This is professional journalism doing its job.

#### When to deploy

Independent Observers are your backbone whenever the question is "what is actually happening, and how do informed people understand it?"

**Corroborating Primary Artifact claims.** A release claims a "leading position" in a market. Search the serious trade and business press for the market plus the organisation's name. You find a piece on consolidation in that market: it lists your subject among "several mid-market players," quotes an analyst saying "the market is fragmented with no clear leader," and puts the subject at roughly 15% share. The "leading position" claim is marketing hyperbole. You then write the balanced version: "While the organisation positions itself as a market leader [Primary Artifact], industry reporting describes a fragmented market with multiple mid-market players, and analyst estimates put its share at approximately 15% [Independent Observer]."

**Understanding trends.** Question: is a stated value genuinely influencing decisions, or is it rhetoric? Collect three pieces from three different outlets with three different angles — one interviewing decision-makers and pricing the premium they pay; one profiling organisations that market the value as a differentiator, with buyer-preference survey data; one on outside entrants underperforming in that market versus comparable markets, with executives acknowledging the resistance. Different reporters, different publications, different angles, consistent theme, specific data in all three: **confidence HIGH** that the factor is genuine and not just marketing.

**Detecting emerging patterns early.** A reporter on a beat notices that three separate interviews mention the same hiring difficulty and two independently mention the same frustration. The pattern surfaces before any formal survey or announcement. By the time associations publish and organisations issue statements, Independent Observers may have had it for months.

#### Characteristic strengths

**Triangulation built in.** Good journalism triangulates by construction: subject perspective, customer perspective, competitor perspective, expert analysis — four viewpoints in one source, already synthesised with editorial judgement.

**Professional verification.** Reporters face consequences for error: reputation damage, editorial discipline, legal liability, destroyed source relationships. That creates an incentive for accuracy promotional sources lack. In action: a release claims "record-breaking growth"; the reporter requests numbers (declined), checks filings (revenue flat), interviews three customers (two switching away), and quotes an analyst on the marketing language. The published line becomes "the organisation claims strong growth, though filings show revenue unchanged from the prior year, and some customers report dissatisfaction." That verification work saves you from taking the release at face value.

**Context and analysis.** A posting reads "Senior specialist, €85,000." The Independent Observer article tells you that is 15% above the regional market rate, that a talent shortage is driving wages up, that three major organisations are chasing the same small pool, and that the contract is only twelve months — suggesting uncertainty about long-term need. Suddenly the posting means something different: a constrained labour market and competitive urgency, not a routine hire.

#### Characteristic weaknesses

**Still one step removed (Proximity 3–4, not 5).** The journalist interviewed people who were in the room; they weren't in the room. Sources chose what to share, the reporter chose what to include, context was lost. "Executives expressed confidence in readiness" does not tell you about the body language suggesting otherwise, the pauses before answers, the questions they wouldn't answer, or the tension between two executives' views. An internal artifact from that meeting might show doubt the article can't capture.

**Deadline pressure and resource constraints.** A typical article runs day one assigned, days two to three interviews, day four written, day five published. What gets missed: deep technical analysis (no time), verification of complex claims (requires expertise), following up contradictions (deadline), comprehensive survey (one reporter, limited reach). Your research, spending weeks on one subject, can go deeper than a journalist spending days.

**The access journalism trap.** Reporters covering a beat build source relationships, and those relationships create pressure. The reporter needs access, early information, background, continuity. The organisation knows critical coverage reduces access and cooperative coverage is rewarded with exclusives. Even good reporters may soften criticism to protect access. **Detect it by:** copy that reads like talking points; no challenging questions; no critical voices; too much "he said" with no "here's what the data shows." Compare coverage across competitors — consistently softer treatment of one suggests a relationship.

**Publication bias — the newsworthiness filter.** Journalism covers what is dramatic, novel or conflictual. Crises, lawsuits, acquisitions, boardroom drama get covered. Steady operational improvement, routine work and normal operations do not. For research this is a real problem: you need to understand normal operations, and Independent Observers over-represent drama and under-represent steady reality.

#### Use cases in practice

**Competitive landscape mapping.** Question: who are the significant players in a market? Search the serious business and trade press over twelve months. One piece names eight vendors, estimates share for the top three, describes two competing approaches, quotes four vendors and three buyers. A second establishes the incumbent at roughly 60% presence, profiles three challengers, notes divergent pricing models, and carries buyer interviews on satisfaction. A third identifies foreign entrants and explains why the market is hard for outsiders. **Synthesis:** dominant incumbent at ~60%; six to eight significant challengers using different approaches; a fragmented market beyond the top player; outside entrants struggling with localisation; two main competitive strategies. **This map came from Independent Observers** — Primary Artifacts (vendor sites) never acknowledge competitors, and Captured Experts (associations) are too diplomatic to name names.

**Understanding narrative shifts.** Question: how has the discussion of an idea evolved? Read the coverage as a timeline. Early period: the idea appears in legal and regulatory context, toned as obligation and cost burden, framed as "organisations must comply." Middle period: it shifts to competitive framing, toned as differentiation opportunity, framed as "organisations use it as an advantage." Recent period: strategic framing, toned as core principle, framed as central to how the sector sees itself. **Pattern:** burden → opportunity → identity over six years. This matters because current discussions are not just a response to recent rules; they sit inside a longer cultural evolution the timeline reveals.

**Detecting red flags through silence.** The subject issues regular releases about partnerships, growth and wins — and Independent Observers have written nothing about them in 24 months. Silence might indicate: too small for attention (scale signal); PR that isn't landing (limited substance); reporters not finding a story (credibility signal); a damaged relationship with the press. Investigate why, check Anonymous Insiders, look for past negative coverage, and assess whether the subject is genuinely unknown or actively avoiding media.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **No named sources.** "Industry sources say… executives familiar with the matter report… people close to the situation indicate…" — all anonymous, no attribution, no verification possible. It might be a reporter protecting sources (legitimate), a reporter with weak sourcing (problematic), or a reporter embellishing (deceptive). You can't tell which; score Verifiability 2–3 rather than 4–5.
- **Article reads like a press release.** Release: "leading provider of innovative solutions delivering unparalleled value through cutting-edge technology." Article: "The organisation, a leading provider of innovative solutions, delivers unparalleled value through cutting-edge technology." That is stenography, not journalism — do not tag it Independent Observer; it is a Primary Artifact distributed through a journalistic channel.
- **Lack of critical perspective.** A balanced piece carries supportive *and* sceptical voices, claims *and* verification, successes *and* challenges. Only-positive, unverified, cheerleading coverage suggests access journalism, insufficient investigation, or promotional content disguised as journalism.
- **Byline or outlet concerns.** Quality markers: real journalist with a history, established publication, evident editorial oversight, a corrections policy. Warning signs: generic bylines, an outlet you've never heard of with no visible editorial standards, small-print "sponsored" or "promoted" labels, and sites designed to look like journalism that are actually content marketing.

### 3. The Promotional Insider

**Character:** Real expertise deployed in service of commercial interests — they know the truth and they're presenting the version that serves their goals, which makes them simultaneously valuable and dangerous.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 4–5 · Timeliness 3–5 · Verifiability 3–4 · Independence 1–2 · Clarity 4–5 · Expertise 4–5 · **Typical total 19–24 (Silver/Bronze Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

Promotional Insiders are domain experts creating content to position their organisation, promote their solution, or generate leads. The vendor white paper explaining why you urgently need their product. The consultancy blog sharing a framework that mysteriously requires their implementation services. The executive thought-leadership demonstrating deep knowledge while steering toward their own offer. The case study showing impressive results while omitting failures.

**The distinctive marker: real knowledge + clear commercial intent.** Once you can see the pattern you will see it everywhere; it is the dominant form of professional content marketing in nearly every field.

Recognition exercise — a piece titled "The Complete Guide to [Requirement]: What [Sector] Needs to Know," published by a vendor, structured as: (1) problem definition — requirements, deadlines, penalties; (2) technical challenges — formats, integration, workflow; (3) common approaches — manual, semi-automated, fully automated; (4) "best practices" which, surprise, require features this vendor offers; (5) a case study of this vendor's customer; (6) "ready to get started? contact us for a demo." That is Promotional Insider content: accurate technical detail wrapped in promotional framing where the solution path leads to their product.

#### When to deploy

They matter when you need to understand what solutions exist, how providers position, and what the technical landscape looks like. **Key insight: despite obvious bias, Promotional Insiders often possess genuine technical depth Independent Observers lack** — the vendor really does understand the architecture better than the journalist; the consultancy really does have patterns from dozens of engagements; the practitioner really has spent fifteen years seeing what generalists miss. The trick is to extract the signal (technical knowledge, operational insight) and filter the noise (exaggerated urgency, competitor dismissal, self-serving framing).

**Mapping the solutions landscape.** Take three sources. Vendor A's white paper: cloud platform, full integration; genuinely useful API architecture, data flow and security model; claims to be the "only comprehensive solution" and "market-leading" — extract the architecture, discard the superlatives. Vendor B's case study: on-premise, modular; genuinely useful implementation timeline, resource requirements, client environment; claims "most cost-effective" and a sovereignty advantage — extract the implementation specifics, verify the cost and sovereignty claims elsewhere. Consultant C's framework: a maturity model; genuinely useful four-stage progression with capability requirements per stage; claims most organisations are at stage one and need consulting to advance — extract the model as an analytical lens, discard the implied necessity. **Synthesis:** three distinct approaches, each described in detail by a party with high proximity and high expertise, giving you a landscape map you could not otherwise assemble. **Cross-verify:** do Independent Observers describe the same approaches; do the named case-study customers exist and confirm the results?

**Understanding technical trade-offs.** Promotional Insiders excel here because they have seen real implementations. A vendor architecture paper will genuinely tell you about extraction difficulties from legacy systems, format transformation across competing standards, validation logic, integration patterns with existing workflow, performance requirements (volume, latency, reliability), and audit-trail requirements. It will also tell you these challenges "require our proprietary approach," that "competitors can't handle the complexity," and that without them you will fail. Use it by noting the listed challenges (likely real — they had to solve them), checking whether multiple vendors name the same challenges (convergence suggests genuine, not invented), verifying through Independent Observer reporting on implementation difficulty, and checking Anonymous Insider sources where practitioners discuss the actual pain.

**Learning terminology and mental models.** A consultancy publishes "The Five Pillars of [Domain] Excellence." Is it objectively correct? No single correct framework exists. Is it useful? Yes — if it reflects how sophisticated players conceptualise the problem. Treat it as one mental model among several; check whether other Promotional Insiders use similar frameworks (convergence suggests industry-standard thinking); note whether Independent Observers or Primary Artifacts have adopted the language (an adoption signal); use it to organise your own categories without letting it bound your thinking. **What you're actually learning is how the market conceptualises the problem space** — which shapes how solutions get designed and how buyers decide.

#### Characteristic strengths

**Deep technical knowledge (Expertise 4–5).** These organisations employ people who built the systems, deployed them dozens of times, troubleshot every edge case, and understand the trade-offs intimately. The expertise is genuine even though it is deployed commercially. A section on data synchronisation in multi-system environments that explains eventual versus strong consistency, conflict resolution strategies, performance implications and when each approach is appropriate is expert-level knowledge — you can learn from it even knowing the conclusion will be "our approach is best."

**High verifiability when done well (3–4).** Good Promotional Insider content carries specific data points, named case studies, technical specifications, and referenced standards. A case study claiming "40% reduction in processing time" that names the customer, states the baseline (200 hours per month, manual), the intervention (implemented in June), the outcome (120 hours per month by September), and the measurement method (time-tracking software) gives you handholds: does the customer exist; do they use this product (check their site, their postings); is a three-month implementation plausible; is 40% realistic against other sources? Even promotional content can be checked.

**Transparent motivation (Clarity 4–5).** Unlike Promotional Authorities, who disguise commercial intent, Promotional Insiders are usually obvious: published on the vendor's site, authored by an employee, ending in "contact us," labelled "solution brief" or "white paper." You know what you're reading, and that transparency lets you adjust interpretation correctly. **Better than "independent research" that is actually funded and disguised.**

#### Characteristic weaknesses

**Systematic bias (Independence 1–2).** Every piece of information is selected and framed to serve the commercial agenda. They are not lying exactly; they are showing you the part of reality that supports their case and omitting everything else.

- **Problem-definition bias.** The problem is defined to fit the solution they sell. A vendor selling an integration platform says "the fundamental challenge is integration across multiple tools." What they don't mention: for some organisations the challenge isn't integration but staff training, or cost, or change management — problems their software doesn't solve.
- **Competitor dismissal bias.** The classic comparison table marks every row a tick for them and a cross or "partial" for competitors. It is usually technically accurate and misleadingly incomplete: features where competitors are stronger are absent, price comparison is absent (it might favour competitors), implementation complexity is absent (a simpler competitor might win), and satisfaction scores are absent (an incumbent might win).
- **Urgency inflation.** "Organisations that don't act now will fall behind." "The crisis requires immediate action." "Only X weeks remain." "Every day of delay increases risk." Reality check: yes, there may be a real deadline — but is it a crisis, and are you genuinely behind if you start today rather than last month, or is this manufactured urgency to shorten the sales cycle?
- **Success metric cherry-picking.** You see "40% efficiency improvement," "30% cost reduction," "99.5% accuracy." You do not see that implementation ran six months over, that it required two extra staff to operate, that it works well only for certain cases, or that three other customers gave up and switched. **The published success is real but not representative.**

#### Use cases in practice

**Technical education, with scepticism.** Read three or four vendor papers on different architectures to acquire vocabulary, basic trade-offs, and — crucially — a map of where vendors *agree* (likely real) versus where they *disagree* (competitive positioning). Then map the landscape: what approaches exist, how do they differentiate, what advantages are claimed. Then verify through other personas: do Independent Observers describe the same approaches; do Academic Authorities discuss similar architectural patterns; do Anonymous Insiders confirm or dispute the claimed trade-offs?

**Understanding positioning.** Vendor A emphasises enterprise-grade, comprehensive, secure — targeting large, security-conscious buyers. Vendor B emphasises quick implementation, ease, cost — targeting smaller, budget-conscious buyers. Vendor C emphasises local engineering, sovereignty, regulatory expertise — targeting the domestic, privacy-conscious buyer. Three positioning strategies reveal how the market is segmented, what buyer concerns vendors perceive, and which position aligns with your subject's priorities.

**Identifying emerging approaches.** Vendors often pioneer before the wider world notices. Content from an early year discusses mostly manual processes; two years later "AI-powered validation" appears as a feature; two years after that it is a baseline assumption and the frontier language has moved to "predictive." The progression is visible through Promotional Insider content over time. **Limitation:** promotional content alone cannot tell you whether "AI-powered" is genuine or a marketing label — verify through technical documentation, Independent Observer technology journalism, Anonymous Insider confirmation from practitioners, and Academic Authority research.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **Unverifiable statistics.** "Studies show our customers achieve 45% average improvement." Which studies? What methodology? What sample? Who conducted it? Published where? **If you can't verify it, discount it entirely** — it might be true, it might be invented.
- **"Independent research" that isn't.** A vendor publishes an "independent study" on ROI; investigation shows the vendor commissioned it, selected the research firm, supplied only its own customers as the sample, and reviewed the findings before publication. That is vendor-funded marketing with a research veneer.
- **Extreme urgency language.** "Critical decision point," "no time to waste," "point of no return." Ask: is the deadline real or manufactured; are competitors emphasising the same urgency; do Independent Observers confirm it; is the urgency designed to prevent comparison shopping? Real urgency exists — manufactured urgency is a sales technique. Learn to tell them apart.
- **Competitor dismissal without substance.** "Unlike legacy solutions that struggle with modern requirements, our approach…" — "legacy" is pejorative without definition, "struggle" is vague with no specific failure named, and old = bad is implied rather than argued. Better competitor discussion names competitors, compares specific features, acknowledges trade-offs in both directions, and shows data. **Vague dismissal suggests weak actual differentiation.**

### 4. The Captured Expert

**Character:** Sophisticated analysis from someone who really knows the domain, but whose funding or organisational mission shapes every conclusion they reach — a peculiar form of credible bias.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 3–4 · Timeliness 3–4 · Verifiability 3–4 · Independence 2–3 · Clarity 3–4 · Expertise 5 · **Typical total 19–23 (Silver/Bronze Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

Captured Experts appear as industry association reports surveying members, consultancy thought leadership sharing frameworks derived from client work, trade publication research funded by the industry it covers, think-tank papers with corporate sponsorship, and academic research with disclosed industry funding.

**The distinctive marker: genuine expertise + rigorous-seeming methodology + structural incentives preventing truly independent analysis.**

A worked recognition: a "2024 Sector Digital Readiness Survey" published by the sector's own professional association, covering 200 organisations, with disclosed methodology, statistical analysis, data visualisation and professional design. **Quality indicators:** real sample size, disclosed method, professional presentation, domain expertise. **Capture indicators:** the association exists to serve and promote members; the questions almost certainly avoid embarrassing them; the findings are framed to present the sector positively; the recommendations favour member interests. That is Captured Expert work.

#### When to deploy

They become valuable when you need aggregated data, benchmarks, or frameworks synthesising patterns across many organisations — information that literally does not exist in other source types.

Suppose you need to know what share of organisations in a sector have implemented something. Primary Artifacts won't tell you (individual organisations treat it as competitive information). Independent Observers don't have it (a journalist cannot survey 200 organisations). Promotional Insiders will inflate it (they're selling the thing). The association actually surveyed 200. **Only the Captured Expert has the access and resources to collect that data.** The trade-off is that you get data unavailable elsewhere, with a bias you must account for.

**Accessing aggregated data.** The survey reports 67% have "begun implementation," 45% feel "well-prepared," an average eight-month timeline, and "technical integration complexity" as the top challenge. Extract the numbers as benchmarks and perception data. Then adjust: is "begun implementation" defined rigorously? Probably not — it likely includes organisations that have barely started, because the association wants to show members are responsive, and "real implementation" versus "had a meeting" is unclear. **Interpretation:** the majority are engaging (real signal), but readiness is likely overstated. **Cross-verify** against posting patterns (if many are hiring for it *now*, that suggests late-stage preparation, not advanced readiness), against Independent Observer reporting on readiness concerns, and against Anonymous Insider reports of implementation chaos behind leadership confidence. **Triangulated conclusion:** "Association survey shows 67% have begun implementation [Captured Expert], though a continued hiring surge [Primary Artifacts] and reporting of preparation gaps [Independent Observer] suggest many implementations remain incomplete as the deadline approaches."

**Understanding industry self-conception.** What does a sector want to believe about itself? A consultancy maturity report — stage one manual and inefficient, stage two digitised, stage three integrated, stage four intelligent, stage five autonomous — claims most organisations sit at stage two and need to reach stage four. Is that objectively accurate? Impossible to say; maturity models are conceptual frameworks, not scientific fact. What it reveals is how consultancies think about progression, what vocabulary they use, what path they recommend, and where they position "the problem" — which is precisely where consulting demand is created. Use it to understand the mental model shaping market discussion, note the pressure it creates to "advance stages," recognise the progression as framing rather than natural law, and treat it as one lens among several.

**Finding proprietary data.** A consultancy can aggregate fifty engagements to identify typical challenges, common failure modes, resource requirements, and timeline realities. What they will not disclose: client identities (confidential), specific client failures (reputational risk), problems the consultancy itself caused (self-interest), and situations where consulting wasn't needed (commercial interest). So: "based on 50 implementations, typical timeline is 6–8 months" is a usable benchmark; "and therefore you need our consulting" is not. Cross-check the benchmark against vendor case studies and Anonymous Insider experience.

#### Characteristic strengths

**Maximum expertise (5).** Associations employ senior practitioners; consultancies hire from top industry roles. A report authored by a former senior partner with 25 years in practice, an association board member of fifteen years, and the chair of a technical standards committee is written by people who genuinely know the domain.

**Aggregated data access.** They are the only source type that can survey hundreds of organisations, aggregate patterns, and present distributions — including cross-tabs no other source can produce, such as implementation status broken out by organisation size (small 45% started, medium 71%, large 89%). That size correlation is valuable intelligence available nowhere else.

**Sophisticated methodology, when disclosed.** Good Captured Expert research discloses sample selection, full survey questions (often in an appendix), response rate, analysis approach, and limitations. A disclosed method — random sample from a 2,500-member association, 200 responses (8% response rate), collected in August, confidence ±7% at 95%, with a note that respondents may not represent non-members or non-respondents — tells you the sample excludes non-members, that an 8% response rate implies respondent bias, that the timing predates peak deadline urgency, and what the precision limits are. **Armed with that, you can interpret the results appropriately.** Transparency about bias is what separates good capture from bad.

#### Characteristic weaknesses

**Structural capture (Independence 2–3).** Not overt corruption — structural positioning that shapes what can be researched and what can be concluded.

- **The association case.** Its purpose is to serve and promote members. So: it won't publish findings that embarrass them; it will frame challenges as external (regulation, technology) rather than internal (member competence); it will emphasise members' positive efforts; and it will recommend solutions that benefit members. Reality: many are unprepared, started late, may miss the deadline. Association framing: "members are actively responding to a challenging regulatory timeline, with the majority engaged in implementation." Both are technically true; the framing moves "unprepared" to "actively responding."
- **The consultancy case.** Its purpose is to generate demand. So: research identifies problems consulting can solve; maturity models position most organisations at the "needs consulting" stage; recommendations suggest professional assistance; success stories feature the consultancy's own approaches. **This isn't lying — it is systematic selection of what to study and how to frame it.**

**Sample selection bias.** Who responds to industry surveys? Engaged members, organisations with time to complete surveys, organisations comfortable with their status — often those doing relatively well. Who doesn't? Struggling organisations too busy surviving, members who have left the association, very small organisations without the resources, and those with problems to hide. **Respondents systematically skew toward the more successful and engaged, so findings overstate sector health.**

**The missing methodology — what isn't asked.** A "digital readiness" survey asks: have you invested in digital tools; do you have a digital strategy; how important is transformation. It does not ask: have your digital investments achieved return; have initiatives failed; are you worse off after investing. Why not? Because the association wants to promote digitalisation and does not want to document it failing. **Result: you learn about intentions and investments, not about outcomes and effectiveness.**

#### Use cases in practice

**Benchmarking with bias adjustment.** Survey says organisations started implementation on average eleven months before the deadline; your subject started at six months. Initial read: behind average. Adjust — does the respondent sample skew larger and better-resourced (yes, by association composition)? Would late starters be less likely to respond (likely — embarrassed organisations avoid surveys)? Does "started implementation" include trivial first steps (probably — vague definitions inflate apparent progress)? **Adjusted read:** six months may be closer to the true median than the inflated average suggests. **Still valuable:** you learned the typical range is 6–11 months.

**Understanding consensus frameworks.** A consultancy's "Four Pillars" — regulatory intelligence, process automation, risk management, continuous improvement. Don't accept it as objective truth; do recognise that convergence across multiple Captured Experts reflects industry consensus thinking; do analyse what it emphasises and what it excludes. This one emphasises technology, process and monitoring, and excludes culture, leadership and change management — implying technical solutions and underweighting human factors. That tells you both how the sector conceptualises the problem *and* what blind spots the framing creates.

**Separating systemic from specific.** A survey's top five implementation challenges — legacy integration (71%), staff training (68%), cost (62%), timeline pressure (59%), vendor selection (43%). If your subject reports a challenge that appears in the top five, it is systemic, not evidence of their incompetence, and your solutions and timelines should account for the structural difficulty. Verify by checking whether Anonymous Insiders elsewhere report the same and whether Independent Observers name it as a sector pain point.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **Vague methodology.** "We surveyed industry leaders" without how many, how selected, when, or what was asked prevents any assessment of quality or bias. Treat findings as directional only, never as precise data.
- **Convenient findings.** "Members are well-prepared, the regulation is reasonable, no major concerns." Too convenient — real situations have problems, tensions and unready actors. Universally positive findings should prompt: are results sanitised; did non-respondents have a different experience; are problems being minimised?
- **Recommendations that align perfectly with organisational interest.** A consultancy concluding that most organisations need external consulting is its business model speaking. It doesn't make the data wrong — it makes the recommendations suspect. **Use the data and the frameworks; ignore the self-serving recommendations.**
- **Missing critical perspectives.** A readiness survey that never asks "do you expect to miss the deadline," "have you considered that implementation may fail," or "are you prepared for penalties" is framed to avoid uncomfortable truths. Their absence is itself a finding.

### 5. The Leaked Document

**Character:** High-value, high-risk — potentially revealing hidden truth, potentially a trap, always requiring extreme caution and extensive corroboration.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 5 · Timeliness varies · Verifiability 4–5 · Independence 1 · Clarity 1–2 · Expertise 4–5 · **Typical total 16–20 (Bronze Tier — but the score dramatically understates both the value and the risk)**

#### Recognition in the field

Leaked Documents appear when internal materials never meant for outside eyes become available: the anonymous leak of a confidential strategy deck; the whistleblower sharing internal projections that contradict public statements; the departing employee posting internal conversations that reveal cultural dysfunction; internal roadmaps that reach a competitor; investigation records that surface what leadership really thought privately.

**The telltale markers: high specificity + unclear provenance.** You're seeing details and language only an insider would have — internal project codenames, specific budget figures, candid leadership comments, unfiltered strategic debate — and you cannot verify who leaked it, why, or whether what you're seeing is complete and unedited versus selectively curated.

A worked recognition: an anonymous email arrives with a deck titled "Q4 Strategic Planning — CONFIDENTIAL — Internal Only," containing a slide reading "Implementation status: behind schedule," a budget breakdown showing €2.3M spent and €1.1M remaining, a risk assessment putting "60% probability we miss the January deadline," and leadership quotes admitting "we underestimated complexity." All the markers are present: internal format, candid language, specific numbers and probabilities, anonymous provenance. Your immediate questions — is it authentic; if authentic, is it complete or edited; who leaked it and why; is it current; is it representative or a worst-case scenario document — **cannot be answered with certainty, which is what makes this persona uniquely difficult.**

#### When to deploy

They become relevant when you are investigating the gap between public narrative and private reality. Public sources show what organisations *want* known; a leak potentially shows what they actually think and do internally. Public: "on track for a successful transition," "proud of our team's diligent preparation," "ready solutions and expertise." Leak: "implementation three months behind schedule," "need to manage client expectations about potential delays," "high risk of missing deadline." The gap reveals public confidence against private concern.

> **Critical protocol: never use a Leaked Document as a sole source. Only use it with explicit corroboration from Gold Tier sources.**

**Hypothesis generation — the correct use.** Step one: note the specific checkable claims (started in June; €2.3M spent; team of eight full-time). Step two: verify them elsewhere — did compliance hiring start around June (Primary Artifacts); is there any journalism on this timeline (Independent Observers); did employees change titles around June (Primary Artifacts)? Step three: assess. If the checkable claims hold, the document gains credibility; if they're wrong, it is fabricated or outdated. Step four: **use the pattern for hypothesis, not the document for proof** — "leaked materials suggest implementation challenges [unverified], which aligns with observed late-stage hiring [Primary Artifacts] and reporting of widespread preparation struggles [Independent Observers]."

**Understanding internal vs. external framing.** Public: "exploring innovative AI solutions for enhanced efficiency." Leak: "the AI project failed its pilot, too inaccurate for production, team recommends the traditional approach." Do not cite the leak. Do use it to direct your questions to other sources: has AI-related hiring stopped (would corroborate the pivot); does any reporting describe traditional versus AI implementations; do partner vendors discuss which approach is in use? If multiple verifiable sources corroborate the pattern, confidence rises.

**When not to touch it at all.** Never use a leak for claims about named individuals ("a leaked email shows the finance chief disagreed") — too risky legally and ethically, unverifiable, and capable of unjustly damaging a person. Never for specific financial data — potentially market-sensitive, possibly legally fraught to hold, and unverifiable without revealing you have the leak. Never for legally sensitive material — possessing it may implicate you, using it may be illegal depending on jurisdiction, and the source's methods raise their own ethical problems. If you encounter such material, note it as private context for your own understanding and do not use or cite it; leave genuinely sensitive leaks to the authorities or to investigative journalists with legal resources.

#### Characteristic strengths

**Maximum proximity (5) when authentic.** These are actual internal artifacts made for internal purposes — no PR filtering, no marketing polish, no reputation management. External language: "leveraging cutting-edge solutions to deliver client value." Internal language: "the software keeps crashing, clients are frustrated, we need to fix this fast." The internal version is more specific, more candid, and more actionable. That authenticity, *if genuine*, is extraordinarily valuable.

**Unfiltered strategic thinking.** Public documents show the strategy you are supposed to know about; leaks potentially show the strategy being actually debated. A single strategy slide listing three options — full automation (high cost, high risk, potential advantage), partial automation (moderate cost, lower risk, matches competitors), maintain manual (low cost, falling behind) — with the note "leadership leaning toward option two: safe, defensible to the board, matches what competitors are doing" reveals the decision framework, the options actually considered, the real reasoning (board defensibility, competitor matching), and the strategic conservatism of choosing safety over advantage. **You would never see that candour in a public-facing source.**

**Verifiable specifics when authentic (4–5).** Internal documents carry project codenames, budget line items, milestones, organisational structure, named roles, and candid progress assessments — more detailed than any public source. **The entire caveat lives in the phrase "when authentic."**

#### Characteristic weaknesses

**Unknown motivation (Independence 1, Clarity 1–2).** You cannot know who leaked or why, which makes bias adjustment impossible. The possibilities: ethical whistleblowing (exposing genuine wrongdoing, accurately representing the situation); personal revenge (a disgruntled individual selectively editing to damage); competitive attack (a rival operative, possibly a fabrication, strategic disinformation); negligent disclosure (accidental, no malice, possibly outdated or a draft); or an *authorised* "leak" (deliberately released, disguised for deniability, a communications move). **You can't tell which, and that makes interpretation nearly impossible without corroboration.**

**Authenticity uncertainty.** With enough technical skill and insider knowledge, convincing forgeries are possible. Fabrication markers: too perfect (contains exactly the smoking gun needed); convenient timing (surfaces at the moment of maximum damage); unusual format (real internal documents have quirks; forgeries are often too clean); internal inconsistencies (dates that don't align, organisational details that are wrong). **But well-made forgeries won't show obvious markers, and authentic documents can look suspiciously convenient by coincidence.**

**Completeness uncertainty.** Even if authentic, you may be seeing selected slides from a longer deck, edited excerpts, a partial chain, or a document stripped of temporal context. You see "60% probability we miss the deadline." You don't see the earlier slide defining what "miss" means (by a day? a month?), the later slide with a mitigation plan that worked, the follow-up meeting where the assessment changed, or the actual outcome. **Selective editing can completely reverse meaning.**

**Legal and ethical exposure.** Depending on jurisdiction and circumstances, possessing leaked material may be illegal, using it may create liability, sharing it compounds the problem, and the source may have obtained it illegally. You can be implicated in trade secret theft, breach of confidentiality, or economic espionage **even though you did not leak it.**

#### Use cases in practice

The only responsible use case is extremely narrow. The protocol:

1. **Don't publicise or share.** If you encounter leaked material, do not spread it. This protects you legally and ethically.
2. **Extract verifiable claims.** Note the factual claims checkable elsewhere: dates, locations, job titles, public figures (not private employees), events that would leave external evidence, organisational structure claims.
3. **Verify independently.** Check every checkable claim against Gold and Silver Tier sources — Primary Artifacts, Independent Observers, Captured Experts.
4. **Use the pattern, not the document.** If verification confirms the pattern, write it from the verified sources: "Posting analysis and industry reporting suggest implementation challenges [cite those]." You do **not** write "according to leaked internal documents…"
5. **Flag uncertainty.** If you cannot verify a claim through other sources, do not use it.

**A worked appropriate use.** The leak claims implementation started in June, a team of eight, behind schedule. Verification: eight employees visibly changed titles to that function around June (Primary Artifacts, public profiles); ongoing postings suggest continued resource need (Primary Artifacts); journalism cites late implementations across the sector (Independent Observer). Your written output: "Analysis of job postings and public professional profiles indicates the organisation began scaling this team in mid-year [Primary Artifacts], with continued hiring suggesting ongoing resource needs [Primary Artifacts]. This timing aligns with reporting of late-stage preparation challenges across the sector [Independent Observer]." **You used the leak to direct the investigation, verified through other sources, and cited only verifiable evidence.**

**A worked inappropriate use.** The leak claims a named finance chief privately opposed the investment and was overruled. You cannot use it: it implicates a specific individual; verification would require asking them, which reveals you hold the leak; if fabricated it damages a reputation; there is no public-interest justification; and the legal and ethical risk is real. **Even if true and relevant, you cannot responsibly use it.**

#### Red flags and cautions

- **Too perfect.** You are investigating whether an organisation is unprepared, and a leak surfaces with leadership admitting they are "woefully unprepared," an assessment calling the deadline "impossible," and an email saying "hide the problems from clients." Suspiciously on-the-nose. Real documents are messier, more ambiguous, and require interpretation.
- **Strategic timing.** The document surfaces at the moment of maximum impact — just before a contract decision, during an investigation, mid-tender, right before a major industry event. Ask who benefits from the timing, whether this is a competitive intelligence operation, and whether it is an authorised "leak."
- **Claims inside knowledge, details are wrong.** The source says they work in the department, but the document uses a title structure the organisation doesn't use, or gets basic terminology wrong. That suggests fabrication by an outsider, a document from a different organisational era, or an exaggerated claim of insider status.
- **No checkable detail verifies.** Codenames appear nowhere else, referenced people don't exist there, dates don't align with known events, figures contradict public financials. Either it is fabricated or it belongs to a different organisation entirely.

> **Final caution: most research never needs Leaked Documents.** If you find yourself relying on leaked material, ask whether you can answer the question without it, whether you have exhausted the other source types, and whether you are reaching for it because it is a convenient shortcut rather than genuinely necessary. In roughly 95% of cases, thorough use of the other personas provides a sufficient foundation without the legal, ethical and verification problems.
>
> **Check the Regulatory Authority first.** Enforcement actions, investigation findings and mandatory disclosures frequently place the same internal facts on the public record — lawfully, citably, and with a named body accountable for them. A regulator's determination does the job a leak would have done, without any of its problems. See §12.

### 6. The Academic Authority

**Character:** Rigorous but slow — the highest methodological standards meeting the lowest timeliness, creating knowledge that is bulletproof but often dated.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 2–3 · Timeliness 2–3 · Verifiability 5 · Independence 5 · Clarity 4–5 · Expertise 5 · **Typical total 23–26 (Gold Tier for foundational knowledge)**

#### Recognition in the field

You are looking at an Academic Authority when you see peer-reviewed journal articles, academic books from university presses, research centre reports with formal methodology, doctoral dissertations, or systematic literature reviews synthesising decades of work. The markers are methodological transparency (a detailed account of how the research was conducted), peer review (journals, conferences, editorial boards), theoretical framing (explicit engagement with existing literature and concepts), and a tone that prioritises precision and qualification over persuasive clarity. These sources read differently — more cautious, more hedged, more concerned with method than with actionable conclusions — because they are written for an audience that will scrutinise the methods before accepting the findings.

#### When to deploy

Academic Authority is essential when you need foundational understanding of slow-moving subjects: how organisational cultures change, what shapes technology adoption over decades, how regulatory frameworks evolve, what the long-run patterns are in industry structure and competitive dynamics. These sources give you the theoretical frameworks and historical context that explain *why* current patterns exist and what forces shape them — even though they cannot tell you what happened last month or what is happening right now in your specific case.

**Practical deployment.** Researching a national business culture around a value such as privacy or sovereignty, you need Academic Authority to understand how that culture emerged historically, what theoretical frameworks explain national differences in such values (institutional theory, cultural dimensions research, the regulatory-state literature), and how business culture and regulatory environment interact over time. The process: search a scholarly index for the topic; filter to reputable journals within the last decade; find the relevant paper; score it (typically Proximity 2, Timeliness 3 because cultural topics decay slowly, Verifiability 5, Independence 5, Clarity 5, Expertise 5 = 25/30); and use it for historical context and theoretical framing, **not** for current conditions.

#### Characteristic strengths

The power is unmatched rigour. **Verifiability scores 5** because methodology is fully disclosed — you know exactly how samples were selected, what was asked, how data was analysed, and what limitations affect interpretation. **Independence scores 5** because peer review and academic norms create accountability commercial sources lack. **Expertise scores 5** because these are specialists who have spent years or decades on narrow domains.

Practically: when a paper says "we interviewed 47 executives about this decision-making," you can read the interview protocol in the appendix, check the sample selection criteria, evaluate whether the sample resembles your subject, assess the analysis method, and read the acknowledged limitations. Compare that to a consultancy report asserting the same conclusion with no disclosed methodology. **You can trust the academic source's specific claims even when it is a few years old, because you can verify how the conclusions were reached.**

#### Characteristic weaknesses

The fundamental limitation is captured in Timeliness (2–3) and Proximity (2–3). Publication lag means two to five years between research and publication — a paper published in 2023 used 2020–2021 data. Proximity suffers because academic research synthesises existing knowledge rather than observing current events directly.

**Real-world impact.** You're researching adoption of a new technology this year, and you find an excellent recent paper on technology adoption in the same professional sector. It is methodologically sound, but: the data was collected before the technology in question existed at scale; the sample was restricted to organisations with 200+ employees while your subject has 85; the sample was drawn from one country and your subject is in another; and the technology measured was the previous generation, not the current one. **The trap:** its Gold Tier score makes you want to cite it, but it is wrong for your specific question. **The correct use:** use it to understand the general theoretical factors affecting adoption in that sector, not to make claims about current adoption rates.

#### Use cases in practice

**Building foundational understanding.** You want to know how organisations in a sector make technology decisions before you can interpret their current behaviour. Seek papers on professional identity and technology adoption (to understand resistance patterns), on institutional isomorphism in professional services (to understand mimetic behaviour — organisations copy each other), and on how regulation shapes technology choice. Don't cite them for current statistics; do use them for frameworks that explain patterns you're seeing in current sources, for understanding why certain factors matter historically, and as theoretical background. **Example integration:** "Job postings reveal that 60% of large organisations in the sector are now hiring for these roles [current source]. This rapid adoption aligns with institutional theory's prediction that professional services firms engage in mimetic isomorphism, copying prestigious peers to maintain legitimacy [Academic Authority]."

**Historical context for culture claims.** You're seeing marketing claims that a value is "core to national business identity." Real, or a recent marketing invention? Search for academic work predating the recent regulation that supposedly created it. If multiple papers from a decade earlier discuss it as a cultural value, the claim has historical validity. If there is no earlier academic discussion at all, "core identity" is likely recent rhetoric.

**When not to use Academic Authority.** You need to know whether organisations are hiring for a role *right now*. Searching for academic papers on hiring is the wrong approach — publication lag will hand you five-year-old data about six-year-old hiring. Use Primary Artifacts (actual postings) and Independent Observers (recent journalism on hiring trends).

#### Red flags and cautions

**Not all academic sources are equal.** Lower-quality markers: publication in pay-to-publish (predatory) journals; no disclosed peer review; authors with no institutional affiliation; vague or missing methodology; no engagement with existing literature; and claims that look designed to attract media attention rather than advance knowledge. **Quality check:** is the journal reputable (impact factor, editorial board); are the authors affiliated with known institutions; does the paper cite other academic work extensively; is the methodology transparent and detailed; do the authors acknowledge limitations?

**The working paper trap.** You find a recent paper on exactly your topic — but look closer: it is a working paper or preprint, not peer-reviewed. Timeliness may be better and methodology may still be disclosed, but quality is unverified. **Score it lower on Verifiability (3–4 rather than 5) until a peer-reviewed version appears.**

**The "doesn't generalise" trap.** Academic research often uses convenience samples. A rigorous paper on "technology adoption in professional services" whose sample is twelve organisations, all in one capital city, all with 1,000+ employees, all self-selected by responding to a recruiting email, may be entirely irrelevant to a 50-person organisation in a regional city. **Rigorous and irrelevant are compatible.**

### 7. The Well-Meaning Generalist

**Character:** Honest but shallow — an enthusiastic outsider who synthesises conventional wisdom and shares accessible insight without the depth to evaluate what they're saying.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 2–3 · Timeliness 3–4 · Verifiability 2–3 · Independence 4–5 · Clarity 4–5 · Expertise 2–3 · **Typical total 17–22 (Bronze/Silver Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

Well-Meaning Generalists are the bloggers who write about everything, the podcast hosts who interview experts across multiple domains, the platform thought leaders sharing observations across industries, the general business authors synthesising trends, and the opinion columnists commenting without deep domain expertise. You recognise them by accessible style (metaphor, analogy and storytelling over technical detail), breadth without specialisation, enthusiasm (more excited about ideas than cautious about limitations), and reliance on secondary sources — they synthesise others' work rather than conducting their own.

#### When to deploy

They become valuable when you need to understand the **mainstream narrative**: what the conventional wisdom is, what stories people are telling each other, which ideas have captured mindshare, and what analogies and frameworks shape how non-experts think about a complex issue. If you're researching whether an anxiety is a real cultural phenomenon affecting decision-making, Well-Meaning Generalist sources reveal that anxiety through their framing and language even though they cannot quantify it rigorously.

**Practical deployment.** You want to know whether interest in a class of tools is driven by genuine regulatory pressure or by generalised anxiety creating demand for control and oversight. The generalist sources reveal the zeitgeist: posts titled "Is [technology] coming for [profession] jobs?"; executives sharing concerns; podcast episodes where generalists interview experts; general-interest coverage in non-specialist publications. Don't cite them for technical facts. Do use them to understand what non-experts believe, analyse their language for emotional framing ("threat," "opportunity," "revolution," "bubble"), and look for patterns across many of them. **Example insight:** "Analysis of 30 general business posts on the topic in one quarter reveals consistent framing of the technology as both inevitable and threatening, with 80% using military metaphors ('arms race,' 'defence,' 'staying ahead'). This suggests the general audience experiences it through an anxiety lens, which may drive demand for control-focused solutions even absent specific regulatory pressure."

#### Characteristic strengths

Their value is the **outsider perspective** (free from industry capture) and **accessibility** (they explain things for non-experts, which reveals how non-expert decision-makers understand the issue). Independence scores 4–5 because they typically have no commercial agenda — they're not selling anything. Clarity scores 4–5 because they're explicit about offering perspective rather than expertise.

**Fresh-eyes observations that insiders miss:** "Why does everyone in this field use the same terminology?" (reveals groupthink). "From outside, the anxiety seems disproportionate." (reveals possible fear-based marketing). "Everyone talks about transformation but the examples are all minor automation." (reveals the gap between rhetoric and reality).

**A worked example of that value.** You're deep in a sector's research, immersed in its jargon and assumptions. A generalist post asks: "Why does everyone act like this regulation is new? It's been in force for years." That naive question reveals that you have been accepting industry framing which treats an ongoing rule as "new pressure," when actually something else — recent enforcement actions? competitive dynamics? — is creating renewed urgency. **The generalist's confusion flags your need to investigate why *now* specifically.**

#### Characteristic weaknesses

Shallow expertise (2–3) and weak verifiability (2–3). Well-Meaning Generalists confidently state things that are wrong, misunderstand technical detail, repeat marketing narratives without recognising them as marketing, overgeneralise from limited examples, and prioritise an interesting story over accurate analysis.

- **Technical misunderstanding.** "This technology will automate the entire profession within two years." Reality: it handles specific tasks; complex judgement remains human. The error is conflating narrow automation with general capability.
- **Repeating vendor marketing.** "Experts say every organisation needs a strategy for this immediately." Reality: the "experts" were vendor representatives selling the strategy work. The error is failing to recognise promotional content and treating it as independent expertise.
- **Overgeneralisation.** "I talked to three startups doing this, so clearly the field is being transformed." Reality: startups aren't representative of established mid-sized organisations. The error is presenting a small, non-representative sample as a trend.

#### Use cases in practice

**Mapping the mainstream conversation.** You want to know what decision-makers are hearing about a topic from their *general* networks rather than from specialists. Collect 20–30 generalist sources from the past six months across mainstream management publications, general podcasts, platform influencers and non-specialist books. Code for common themes, metaphors and recommendations; note what they consistently get wrong relative to specialist sources; identify the conventional wisdom shaping non-expert thinking. **Insight:** "Mainstream discourse frames this as an existential competitive threat requiring immediate response, while specialist sources emphasise measured adoption focused on specific use cases. That gap suggests leaders may face pressure from boards influenced by generalist sources to move faster than operational reality warrants."

**Finding accessible entry points.** Before diving into specialist material on a complex topic, use explainer posts, an interview episode with a real expert, or a "five things to know" thread to acquire vocabulary and concepts, identify the questions worth deeper investigation, and harvest their references to specialist sources. Then move up. **Never use them for statistical claims, technical specifications, strategic recommendations, or as evidence in a final output.**

**Understanding the client or constituent perspective.** If your subject sells to or serves people who are not specialists in the subject matter, generalist sources reveal how those people think: what analogies they use (how they conceptualise the unfamiliar), what concerns dominate (their emotional drivers), what solutions sound appealing (buying criteria shaped by accessible framing). Strategic application: speak the language of generalist sources in communication, while building on specialist sources for actual requirements.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **The confident-ignorance trap.** Generalists often write with a confidence that masks shallow understanding, and confident tone ≠ accurate information. Warning signs: sweeping unhedged claims with dates attached; no specialist sources cited, only other generalists; complexity dismissed ("it's simple, just…"); no acknowledgement of uncertainty.
- **Viral misinformation risk.** Incorrect generalist content spreads faster than accurate specialist content because it is more accessible and more dramatic. The cascade: vendor marketing asserts a statistic with no methodology → a generalist repeats it without checking → other generalists share that post → "everyone knows" a number that traces back to an unverified promotional claim. **Your obligation: verify every statistic back to its original source, not to where you first saw it.**
- **The "democratised expertise" fallacy.** Access to information is not the ability to evaluate information; reading about a practice is not understanding the practice; summarising others' expertise is not possessing expertise. **Use generalists for zeitgeist, never for technical fact.**

### 8. The Anonymous Insider

**Character:** Potentially valuable, probably biased, impossible to verify — claims insider knowledge while hiding behind anonymity, creating maximum uncertainty about both motivation and accuracy.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 4–5 · Timeliness 4–5 · Verifiability 2–3 · Independence unknown · Clarity 1–2 · Expertise 3–5 · **Typical total 15–20 (Bronze Tier, high uncertainty)**

#### Recognition in the field

Anonymous Insiders appear on employer review sites, workplace-anonymous apps, industry subreddits, anonymous accounts claiming inside knowledge, forum comments from unverified professionals, and anywhere someone shares workplace or industry information while protecting their identity. You recognise them by the combination of insider-seeming detail — specific references to culture, project names, organisational dynamics — with complete anonymity: no way to verify identity, credentials, or even that they work where they claim.

#### When to deploy

They become relevant when you need to understand internal culture, employee experience, workplace dynamics, or any aspect of organisational reality that no official source will discuss honestly. No organisation publishes a Primary Artifact saying "our culture is toxic" or "leadership doesn't follow our stated values." Independent Observers rarely get access to internal dynamics absent a crisis or a legal case. Current employees won't speak on record about problems because of career risk. **Anonymous sources are sometimes the only window into certain organisational truths.**

> **Critical protocol: never use a single anonymous source as evidence. Only use patterns across five or more anonymous sources, and even then only as hypothesis generators for verification by other means.**

**Practical deployment.** An organisation's site emphasises "work-life balance" and a "supportive culture." Search employer review sites (aim for at least ten reviews before reading any pattern), workplace apps if the organisation is large enough, and relevant community forums. Look for what repeats, whether anonymous sources agree with each other, and whether claims are specific enough to be credible. A worked pattern across fifteen reviews over four years: 11/15 mention long hours in peak season; 8/15 mention supportive direct managers; 6/15 mention a disconnect between stated values and senior behaviour; 3/15 affirm good work-life balance. **Interpretation:** supportive direct management appears consistently and is likely real; long peak-season hours contradict the balance claim; only three of fifteen affirm the marketing. **Conclusion:** team-level management is supportive (validated by pattern); the work-life balance claim is aspirational rather than descriptive (contradicted by pattern); long peak hours are a structural reality (validated by pattern).

#### Characteristic strengths

Used correctly, they provide access to information available nowhere else. Proximity 4–5 because they claim, and may actually have, insider access. Timeliness 4–5 because anonymous platforms enable real-time reporting. Expertise varies 3–5 with role and tenure.

**What they reveal.** Culture reality against marketing — official "we value diversity" versus an anonymous pattern noting the actual composition of senior ranks: aspiration stated, reality not yet achieved. Operational dysfunction — official silence versus an anonymous pattern that several major clients left over missed deadlines: problems affecting retention. Leadership gaps — official bios emphasising credentials versus an anonymous pattern that leadership doesn't understand modern technology: a potential vulnerability to more capable competitors.

#### Characteristic weaknesses

They score terribly on Independence (unknown — could be 1, could be 5, you can't tell) and Clarity (1–2, motivation completely hidden). The fundamental problem is you can't know who is speaking or why, which makes bias adjustment impossible.

**The unknown-motivation problem.** An anonymous review says "toxic culture, incompetent leadership, everyone's leaving." Possible realities: a truth-teller exposing genuine dysfunction (you'd want to know); a person dismissed for poor performance (biased revenge); a competitor's employee spreading disinformation; a rejected applicant with a grievance; a former employee describing a culture that has since changed (outdated). **You can't tell which, which makes the source nearly useless in isolation.**

**The sampling-bias problem.** Who writes anonymous reviews? People angry enough to spend the time (negative bias), people delighted enough to help recruiting (positive bias), and people with strong opinions generally (extremity bias). Who doesn't? Most people (satisfied enough not to bother), the busy (often the successful), and those who left on good terms. **Anonymous sources are systematically biased toward the extremes and are not representative of average experience.**

#### Use cases in practice

**Culture due diligence, pattern-based.** Protocol: collect a minimum of twenty anonymous data points across platforms; time-filter to the last two years (culture changes); separate by level (junior and senior perspectives differ); code for themes and count what appears ten or more times. Worked example, checking an "innovative culture" claim: 3/20 mention innovative approaches (minority); 14/20 mention traditional methods, "old school," or resistance to change (majority pattern); 2/20 describe a specific innovation (weak signal). **Conclusion:** the claim is not supported by the anonymous pattern; investigate further through other source types.

**Red flag detection.** Anonymous sources aren't proof, but they identify questions worth asking. If 8/15 reviews mention an unclear career path and favouritism in promotion, that doesn't prove favouritism — it suggests you should check whether promotion criteria are stated in postings (Primary Artifacts), analyse actual promotion patterns by tenure and background on public professional profiles, and look for any journalism about turnover. If multiple source types corroborate, confidence rises.

**When not to use them at all.** Never for specific factual claims you can't verify elsewhere; never for negative claims about named individuals; never for financial performance; never for technical capability or limitation; never for legal or regulatory compliance. Anonymous sources could be fabricated, personally motivated, or years out of date — **using them for anything checkable by other means is research malpractice.**

#### Red flags and cautions

- **The coordinated attack pattern.** Five or more negative reviews appear within a fortnight with similar language and themes. Either a genuine crisis prompted an exodus and a burst of review-writing, or this is coordinated. Investigate: is any crisis being reported (Independent Observers); are there Primary Artifact signals (leadership change, layoffs, lost contracts); does the timing coincide with a competitive event (a pitch, a merger, an investigation)?
- **The too-specific-to-be-real pattern.** A review contains detail that would identify the writer if authentic — "I worked directly with [named person] on the [named] account in 2023, and he told me privately that…" If true, the writer is identifiable, which defeats the purpose of anonymity; more likely it is fabrication by someone with limited insider knowledge trying to sound credible. **Real anonymous insiders are usually somewhat vague on identifying details, precisely to protect themselves.**
- **The eternal grudge.** A reviewer with ten or more posts over several years, all negative, all about the same organisation, indicates personal obsession rather than representative experience. Exclude from pattern analysis.

### 9. The Tertiary Compiler

**Character:** Useful starting point but dangerous endpoint — aggregates information from other sources without adding analysis, creating convenient but often oversimplified or outdated summaries.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 1 · Timeliness 2–3 · Verifiability 2–3 · Independence 3–4 · Clarity 4–5 · Expertise 2–3 · **Typical total 14–18 (Bronze/Questionable Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

Tertiary Compilers include encyclopedia entries, organisation-profile databases, industry directories, AI-generated summaries, news aggregators, and "ultimate guide" posts compiling information from many places. You recognise them by their lack of original observation (everything is sourced from elsewhere), their consistent formatting across many subjects (template-driven), their encyclopedic tone (neutral summary rather than analysis), and their explicit purpose as reference material rather than original research.

#### When to deploy

Tertiary Compilers serve exactly one purpose: **rapid orientation to unfamiliar territory before deeper research begins.** Use them to learn basic vocabulary, identify which questions matter, map the landscape of available information, and find references to primary sources worth investigating. Then immediately move beyond them.

> **The 15-minute rule: spend a maximum of fifteen minutes with Tertiary Compilers, then move to higher-quality source types.**

**Practical deployment.** You're assigned a subject in a sector and a regulatory area you know nothing about. Phase one, fifteen minutes, Tertiary Compilers only: the encyclopedia entry on the regulatory concept (basic definition, that it is regulation-driven, that it is regional rather than national) and on the sector (the distinctions between professional titles that outsiders confuse, the major players) — then follow the references out to the actual regulatory sources and associations. Organisation profiles: look up five major players; note size, locations, stated specialisations, recent public statements. A funding or company database: search the vendor category; identify major vendors, recent funding, stated focus. An AI summary: ask for an overview of key challenges; use it to spot terms you need to learn and claimed key issues. **Phase one output:** basic vocabulary acquired, key players identified, major questions surfaced, ready to move on. **Phase two — the actual research — uses no Tertiary Compilers at all.**

#### Characteristic strengths

They excel at breadth, consistency and accessibility, covering vast ranges of topics in standardised formats that make information easy to find and compare across subjects. Clarity scores 4–5 because the purpose is explicit (reference compilation), and Independence is moderate at 3–4 because they usually have no commercial agenda beyond being reference material.

**Good for:** quick checkable facts (founding date, location, size as of the last update, sector); landscape mapping (an incomplete but usable list of major players, common terms and acronyms, related concepts, links to official sources); and **sanity checking** — if your research contradicts the encyclopedia entry, either you've found something the compilers haven't captured (good) or you've made a mistake (check your work).

#### Characteristic weaknesses

The fundamental limitation is Proximity = 1 (no original observation), combined with information decay (updated irregularly) and quality variance (some entries excellent, others wrong or misleading). You're seeing whatever the compilers chose to include based on what was easily available, not necessarily what is true or important.

- **Information age decay.** A profile says 50 employees. That figure is five years old; the real number is now 85. And the last-update date is unclear or missing.
- **Selective inclusion.** The entry lists the "major" organisations in a field and omits three mid-sized ones that actually dominate the segment you care about — because the compiler didn't know about them or judged them not major.
- **Error propagation.** Source A gets a fact wrong. Compiler B copies A. Compiler C copies B. The error now appears in three places and looks corroborated.
- **AI fabrication.** An AI summary invents plausible-sounding facts, cites non-existent sources, and fabricates statistics with total fluency.

#### Use cases in practice

**Correct use — starting point only.** Step one: the encyclopedia entry tells you the mandate takes effect in a given month and applies to a given class of transaction, and its references cite official government sources. Bookmark those. Step two: read the actual regulation from the official source and get accurate dates, requirements and exceptions. Step three: find expert analysis — Independent Observer coverage of impact, Captured Expert association guidance. **Never cite a Tertiary Compiler in the final output. Use it to find better sources, then cite those.**

**Incorrect use — lazy endpoint.** "According to [encyclopedia], the mandate takes effect in January. Most organisations are prepared." Four problems: the compiler isn't a citable source under most research standards; "most are prepared" is an unsourced or outdated claim; nothing was verified against the authoritative source; and no current reporting was sought. **The correct version:** "The mandate takes effect on [date] [cite: official regulation]. Recent industry reporting suggests significant preparation gaps [cite: named publication, date]."

**AI summaries — a special case.** They are Tertiary Compilers with unique problems. **Use for:** initial orientation and question formulation. **Never use for:** facts, citations, or anything in a final output. Correct workflow: ask for common challenges in an area; treat the returned list as a hypothesis generator; search for actual sources discussing each item; verify which are real through Independent Observers or research; discard the unverified. Incorrect workflow: copy the list into the output, treat the explanations as facts, cite the model as a source.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **The "anyone can edit" problem.** Open encyclopedia quality varies dramatically by topic. High-traffic topics are generally accurate and heavily monitored; low-traffic topics — small organisations, niche industries — can be wrong, rarely updated and barely overseen. Check the last edit date (recent edits suggest active monitoring), the talk page (are facts disputed?), and the citations (does it cite quality sources you can verify?).
- **The database decay problem.** Profile databases are only as current as their last update. A profile showing revenue, headcount and "active" status from five years ago may describe an organisation that has since been acquired, now operates as a subsidiary, and has doubled in size. **Always verify key facts through Primary Artifacts or recent Independent Observer sources.**
- **The aggregator bias problem.** News aggregators select by algorithms optimised for engagement, not importance, so controversial stories are over-represented and boring-but-important stories are under-represented, skewing your view of reality toward drama. **Don't let an aggregator tell you what matters — use active search for specific questions.**

### 10. The Promotional Authority

**Character:** Expert turned salesperson — genuine knowledge deployed in service of commercial interests, wrapped in educational framing that masks the sales pitch.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 3–4 · Timeliness 3–4 · Verifiability 3–4 · Independence 1–2 · Clarity 3–4 · Expertise 4–5 · **Typical total 18–23 (Silver/Bronze Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

Promotional Authorities are the industry analysts who "coincidentally" favour the companies that sponsor their research, the expert speakers at vendor-sponsored conferences, the thought leaders whose frameworks require their services, the "independent" research firms funded by the vendors they evaluate, and the consultants whose educational content steers toward their commercial offering. You recognise them by real expertise combined with commercial motivation — their income depends on favourable positioning — wrapped in educational framing (webinars, frameworks, analyst reports, thought leadership) that obscures the promotional intent.

> **The key distinction from the Promotional Insider: Promotional Authorities claim or imply independence while actually being commercially captured. Promotional Insiders are transparently promotional from the start.**

#### When to deploy

They matter when you need to understand how a market positions itself and which frameworks dominate industry thinking — particularly around solutions, "best practices" and de facto standards. Despite compromised independence they often possess genuine expertise and create genuinely useful frameworks, and those frameworks matter because they shape how your subject and its competitors think about their own challenges.

> **The trick: extract knowledge while rejecting conclusions.**

**Practical deployment — maturity models.** Major consultancies publish maturity frameworks assessing how advanced organisations are. **Extract the structure:** which dimensions do they assess (what experts consider important); what progression do they describe (adoption paths); what terminology do they use (market language). **Reject the self-serving elements:** the "therefore you need consulting" conclusion; the urgency framing; the assessment tool that positions most organisations in an early stage, which is exactly what creates demand. A worked case: a four-stage model (manual → digitised → integrated → predictive) across four dimensions (process automation, data analytics, client experience, staff capability), with transition challenges named at each stage. You extract the stages, dimensions and transition challenges. You ignore the claim that 68% of organisations are stuck at stage one, the recommendation to invest urgently, and the case studies that all showcase the consultancy's own projects. **How you write it:** "Industry frameworks describe digital maturity progressing through process automation, then data integration, then predictive capability [cite framework]. Job posting analysis suggests organisations in this sector currently sit at the automation stage — 64% of digital-related positions emphasise process efficiency versus 12% emphasising predictive analytics [Primary Artifacts]." You cited the framework for its conceptual structure, established the stage placement independently, and avoided repeating the self-serving urgency.

#### Characteristic strengths

Genuine Expertise (4–5) combined with high production quality, sophisticated frameworks, and access to proprietary data or market research. They invest heavily in research because it serves their commercial model, which means their knowledge products can be more comprehensive and polished than genuinely independent research that lacks comparable resources.

- **Industry terminology and frameworks:** how sophisticated players conceptualise problems; what language dominates professional discourse; what mental models shape decisions.
- **Market positioning intelligence:** how vendors differentiate; what capabilities are table stakes versus differentiators; which trends are being marketed hardest.
- **Aggregated insight:** data across many engagements, pattern recognition across many implementations, comparative benchmarks (with caveats about sample bias).

**A worked example of legitimate value:** a major analyst firm's vendor-landscape quadrant. **Compromised elements:** vendors pay to be evaluated (inclusion bias); the methodology favours certain vendor types; leader placement correlates with vendor spend. **Genuine value despite the bias:** a comprehensive vendor landscape in one place; consistent evaluation criteria across vendors; a clear view of how vendors position themselves. **How to use it:** treat it as a market map of positioning, not as an objective quality assessment, and verify specific claims elsewhere.

#### Characteristic weaknesses

The Independence score of 1–2 reflects that the entire business model depends on maintaining relationships with vendors, sponsors or clients whose interests shape the research questions, the methodology, the emphasis of findings, and the recommendations. **This isn't subtle bias — it is structural capture that ensures the research can never threaten the commercial relationships funding it.**

- **The funding mechanism.** Research firms take subscription fees from vendors or sponsorship for specific reports. Consultancies publish frameworks that create consulting demand. Expert speakers are paid by vendors to present at their events. Analysts earn revenue from vendor subscriptions and inquiry services.
- **The subtle shaping.** Not through lying, but through which questions get researched (avoid the inconvenient), how samples are selected (exclude unfavourable cases), which findings get emphasised (highlight positives), and what recommendations follow (favour sponsor approaches).
- **A worked example of structural capture.** An analyst firm researches "adoption barriers" for a technology. The question they ask: what prevents organisations from adopting it? The question they never ask: do organisations that adopted it actually see a return? The second question goes unasked because the vendors fund the research and a negative return finding would threaten that funding. **The result: research that "objectively" identifies barriers while never questioning whether overcoming them is worth it.**

#### Use cases in practice

**Decoding vendor positioning.** An analyst market guide lists 25 vendors with brief descriptions, organises them by type (point solution versus platform), and notes each vendor's claimed differentiators. Use it to get a comprehensive vendor list (saving you the legwork), understand how the market segments itself, and note what each vendor wants to be known for — then verify everything elsewhere before believing it. **What not to do:** trust their evaluation of which vendors are "best"; believe capability claims unverified; assume the listed set is the complete market (unlisted vendors exist); or cite the report as proof that a vendor is qualified.

**Learning industry frameworks.** A consultancy publishes a five-stage maturity model (reactive → managed → standardised → predictive → optimised). Learn the framework, then apply it as an analytical lens: use the stages to organise your observations, but **determine stage placement through other sources rather than through the consultancy's own self-assessment tool.** Written form: "Primary Artifact analysis (job postings) and Independent Observer reporting suggest the subject operates at the 'managed' stage, hiring for process roles rather than predictive analytics capability." You used the framework as structure and reached the placement independently.

**When not to trust them at all.** Competitive assessments ("Vendor A is a Leader, Vendor B a Challenger") may reflect vendor spending on analyst access rather than capability. Urgent recommendations ("act now or fall behind") create demand and may not reflect genuine urgency. Market sizing ("this market will reach $X billion") is inflated to serve vendor interests in attracting investment. Capability claims ("reduces work by 70%") rest on cherry-picked deployments, not typical results.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **The "independent research" deception.** A firm claims independence while being funded by the vendors it evaluates: the study is funded by three of them, the sample includes only successful adopters, organisations that tried and failed were not studied, and the methodology was designed to favour the outcome. That is vendor-funded marketing with a research veneer.
- **The pay-to-play analyst model.** Major analyst firms charge vendors to be included in research, to access analysts for "inquiries," and to reuse reports in marketing. Vendors who don't pay aren't evaluated (exclusion); vendors who pay more get more attention (bias); critical findings about paying vendors get softened (capture). **The result is that analyst reports serve vendors more than buyers.**
- **The expert-for-hire problem.** An "independent expert" speaks at a vendor's conference, writes for the vendor's blog, and joins the vendor's webinar. Are they paid? Usually. Does that create obligation? Yes, even unstated. Can they be critical of a sponsor? Not while maintaining the relationship.
- **The sophisticated version.** The expert never explicitly promotes anyone, but their framework, terminology and recommendations "coincidentally" align with exactly what one vendor offers — a talk on "five imperatives," all five of which happen to be the sponsoring vendor's capabilities, with competitor capabilities unmentioned. **That is promotional content masked as expert education.**

### 11. The Direct Witness

**Character:** Someone who personally saw, did, or lived the thing and says so in their own name — the most immediate account available, and the one with the narrowest field of view.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 5 · Timeliness varies · Verifiability 2–3 · Independence 2–3 · Clarity 3–5 · Expertise 2–4 · **Typical total 17–23 (Bronze/Silver Tier — the score consistently understates the value of the specifics and overstates the reliability of the interpretation)**

#### Recognition in the field

You are looking at a Direct Witness when a named person describes something they were present for, in the first person, without a professional obligation to anyone else's account of it. The customer writing a detailed review of what actually happened when they used the product. The former employee publishing a signed account of a reorganisation they sat through. The resident who attended the planning hearing and wrote up what was said. The participant in a trial describing their own side effects. The engineer who wrote the migration and posted about how it went. The signed affidavit or testimony given under a name. The oral-history interview with the person who was in the room.

**The distinctive markers: first person + named + present.** All three must hold. Drop the first person and you have a reporter (Independent Observer). Drop the name and you have an Anonymous Insider. Drop the presence and you have someone repeating what they heard, which is a Tertiary Compiler with a byline.

The line against Primary Artifact is worth holding precisely, because the two are constantly confused. A Primary Artifact is a *thing the subject produced* — a filing, a posting, a release — and it carries the subject's institutional voice. A Direct Witness is *a person accounting for an experience*, and the voice is personal, sequential and sensory. An organisation's incident report is a Primary Artifact. The engineer's own signed write-up of the same incident is a Direct Witness. They will not say the same thing, and the difference between them is often the finding.

#### When to deploy

Direct Witnesses matter when the question is what it was actually like, what actually happened in a specific instance, or whether an official account survives contact with someone who was there.

**Testing an official account against lived experience.** An organisation's own account of a service change says it was "implemented smoothly with minimal customer disruption" [Primary Artifact]. Six named customers publish detailed accounts of the same fortnight: three describe a specific failure at the same step; two describe the same workaround they were told to use; one describes no problem at all. You have not disproved the official account — "minimal" is undefined — but you now know the failure mode, the step it occurs at, and that the organisation's own language conceals it. **The Direct Witness supplies the specific; the specific is what other personas can then be pointed at.**

**Recovering detail that summaries strip out.** Aggregate sources tell you a policy was implemented. A Direct Witness tells you the queue formed at 6am, the form required a document nobody had been told to bring, and staff were turning people away by mid-morning. That texture is not decoration — it is the operational mechanism the aggregate hides, and it generates checkable questions (was the document requirement published? when? by whom?) that you take to Primary Artifacts and Regulatory Authorities.

**Establishing that something occurred at all.** Where no institution recorded an event — an unminuted meeting, an informal instruction, a practice nobody wrote down — a named first-hand account may be the only evidence it existed. Treat this as the weakest legitimate use, and never on a single witness.

> **Protocol: one Direct Witness establishes a specific, never a pattern.** Two witnesses who were present at the same event and independently describe the same detail is corroboration. Two witnesses describing similar experiences at different times and places is a hypothesis, not a pattern — take it to a Captured Expert's aggregated data or an Independent Observer before you call it one.

#### Characteristic strengths

**Maximum proximity (5), unmediated.** Nobody chose what to include on the witness's behalf. No editor cut the paragraph, no communications team softened the verb, no analyst compressed three hours into a sentence. Where a journalist reports that "customers described the rollout as difficult," the witness tells you which screen, which error, which day, and what the support line said.

**Accountability of the name.** Unlike an Anonymous Insider, a Direct Witness has attached their identity to the account and carries reputational and sometimes legal exposure for getting it wrong. That is a real constraint — not proof of accuracy, but a materially different incentive from anonymous posting, and the reason a signed account scores Verifiability 2–3 where an anonymous one scores lower and stays pattern-only.

**Specificity you can actually check.** Good witness accounts are dense with dates, sequences, named steps and quoted instructions. Every one of those is a handhold: did the outage window match, was the form requirement published, does the timeline align with a filing. **The witness's interpretation is not checkable; the witness's particulars usually are.** Separate the two on intake.

**Consequence made visible.** Witnesses report what the change cost them — hours, money, a missed deadline, a treatment stopped. Institutional sources describe changes; witnesses describe effects. For any question about impact, that is the persona holding the evidence.

#### Characteristic weaknesses

**Memory is reconstructive, not stored.** People do not replay recordings; they rebuild accounts each time they tell them, and the rebuild is shaped by what they have learned since, by what they have already said publicly, and by how the story has landed with previous audiences. Confidence rises with retelling while accuracy does not. **An account given a week after the event and an account of the same event given three years later are different-quality sources, and the later one will usually sound better.** Always record when the account was given relative to the event, and prefer the earlier telling.

**One vantage point, presented as the view.** The witness saw one part of it. They did not see the other four rooms, the decision that preceded the instruction, or the ninety cases that went normally. A witness who queued for four hours honestly reports a four-hour queue and cannot tell you it was the worst hour of the worst day. **Witness accounts systematically read as more typical than they are, because the witness has no comparison set.**

**No obligation to context.** An Independent Observer is expected to note the counter-view; a witness is not, and generally does not. Nothing in the form of the source requires the witness to say who disagrees, what the organisation's explanation was, or what happened next. That absence is structural, not dishonest — but it means a witness account is never balanced by construction, and you must supply the balance yourself.

**Investment in their own version.** Most people who take the trouble to publish a first-hand account have a reason: they were harmed, they were vindicated, they are in dispute, they want it on the record before someone else's version sets. Sometimes there is a claim pending, a settlement in view, or a book. That does not make the account false — the harmed party often knows the most — but it does mean the account was shaped for a purpose, and you should identify the purpose before you weigh the content.

**Unfalsifiable in both directions.** "I was told this in a meeting with no minutes" cannot be proven and cannot be disproven. This cuts both ways, and the second way is the one researchers forget: a denial by the organisation is equally unverifiable. Do not treat an official denial as settling a witness claim, and do not treat a witness claim as settled by its own vividness. **Where a claim is unfalsifiable, report it as contested and move to what is checkable.**

#### Use cases in practice

**Failure-mode identification.** Question: why do users abandon a process partway through? Collect twelve signed first-hand accounts across the platforms where they appear. Nine name the same step. Seven describe the same missing information at that step. Four describe the same unsuccessful workaround. **Synthesis:** a specific, locatable failure at a named step, with the mechanism described. **Still needed:** Primary Artifacts (does the documentation actually omit this?), Captured Expert or operator data (what share of users hit it?), Independent Observer (has anyone reported it as a systemic issue?). The witnesses told you *where to look and what to look for*; they cannot tell you how common it is.

**Reconstructing an undocumented sequence.** Question: in what order did a decision get made? Three named participants give accounts. Two agree on the order and one differs on the placement of a single step; all three agree on who was present and on what was decided. Write it as: the sequence is corroborated by two participants and disputed on one point by a third [attribute each], with the attendance and the outcome consistent across all three. **You have written what the evidence supports and marked the seam.** That is the correct output shape for witness-based reconstruction — never a smoothed single narrative.

**When *not* to rely on a Direct Witness.** Question: how widespread is a problem? A witness account cannot answer this and will feel as though it does, which is the trap. Nor can witnesses establish causation ("the change caused the outcome" — they observed a sequence, not a mechanism), nor rates, shares, averages or trends. For prevalence you need aggregated data (Captured Expert), systematic observation (Independent Observer), or the institutional record (Primary Artifact, Regulatory Authority).

#### Red flags and cautions

- **Detail that arrives too late.** Compare the account given at the time with the account given after a dispute began. Specifics that appear only in the later version — a remembered quote, a newly precise date, an added participant — are the signature of reconstruction under pressure. Note the gap and weight the earlier version.
- **Novelistic precision about the distant past.** Verbatim dialogue from a meeting eleven years ago, complete with hesitations, is not what memory produces. It may be substantially true in substance and it is not evidence of the words. Treat quoted speech from old accounts as paraphrase in every case.
- **The account is doing a job.** The write-up ends in a fundraiser, a legal appeal, a job search, a launch, or a subscription. This does not disqualify it — the person with the grievance is often the person with the information — but read the framing knowing what the piece is for, and check whether the checkable particulars survive independent verification.
- **Presence claimed but not evidenced.** "I was there when they decided" from someone whose public record shows no connection to the organisation at the relevant time. Verify the claim of presence itself through Primary Artifacts (public professional history, membership lists, attendance records) before you weigh anything they say about it.
- **Suspicious convergence of wording.** Several independent witnesses using the same distinctive phrasing suggests they read each other, were coordinated, or are drawing on a common secondary account. Genuine independent witnesses agree on facts and diverge on language. **Identical language across accounts reduces corroborative value rather than increasing it.**

### 12. The Regulatory Authority

**Character:** A body with statutory power putting a finding on the record — procedurally constrained, publicly accountable, consequential when wrong, and blind to everything outside its remit.

**Typical scoring pattern:** Proximity 3–4 · Timeliness 1–3 · Verifiability 5 · Independence 4–5 · Clarity 4–5 · Expertise 4–5 · **Typical total 22–27 (Gold/Silver Tier)**

#### Recognition in the field

Regulatory Authorities are the bodies that can compel, license, fine, prohibit or certify, publishing in their own name: a competition authority's decision on a merger; a data-protection regulator's enforcement notice; a financial supervisor's licence register or sanction; an audit office's report on how public money was spent; an inspectorate's grading of a facility; a standards body's determination that a product does or does not conform; a court's judgment; a central bank's statistical release; a medicines agency's approval or withdrawal; a company or land register.

**The distinctive marker: a determination backed by power.** Not "we think" but "we have found, and the following consequences follow." You recognise it by the procedural furniture — a case or reference number, a stated legal basis, a named decision-maker or panel, a description of the process followed, a right of appeal, an effective date.

The line against Primary Artifact is the one that matters most and the one most often blurred. **A filing lodged with a regulator is a Primary Artifact** — the subject wrote it, in its own voice, to be seen. **The regulator's determination about that filing is a Regulatory Authority** — a different body, adversarial to the subject's framing, with power attached. A company's annual accounts submitted to a register: Primary Artifact. The register's rejection of them, or the supervisor's finding that they were misstated: Regulatory Authority. The same document set can produce both tags, and telling them apart is the whole point of the distinction.

The line against Academic Authority is cleaner: both are rigorous, but the discipline is different in kind. Academic rigour is enforced by peer review and reputation among specialists. Regulatory rigour is enforced by law, procedure and the prospect of the finding being overturned on appeal. **Peer review punishes bad method; legal process punishes bad findings.** The second constraint is narrower and harder.

#### When to deploy

Regulatory Authorities are essential wherever you need a fact that is on the record rather than asserted, and wherever an institution has been made to disclose something it would not have disclosed voluntarily.

**Establishing hard baselines.** Dates a rule takes effect, thresholds that trigger an obligation, who holds which licence, what a facility was rated, whether an entity exists and since when, who its directors are, what penalty was imposed and for what. These are the facts that every other persona will paraphrase inaccurately. Go to the source: no Tertiary Compiler summary of a regulation belongs in an output when the determination itself is published.

**Getting at compelled disclosure.** This is the persona's unique power. Regulators can require what nobody would volunteer: the incident that had to be reported within 72 hours, the remediation plan submitted under an undertaking, the internal figures produced during an investigation and quoted in the decision. **An enforcement decision often contains, in its recitals, internal facts that would otherwise only ever surface as a Leaked Document — lawfully, attributably, and citably.** When you find yourself reaching for a leak, check first whether a regulator has already put the same material on the record.

**Resolving contested claims.** An organisation says it holds an authorisation; the register says it does not, or holds a different one, or holds one that lapsed. A vendor claims certification; the certifying body lists no such certificate. These checks are fast, decisive and repeatedly productive — and they are the correct way to test a Promotional Insider's or Promotional Authority's credential claims.

**Reading the enforcement pattern.** A single decision tells you about one entity. A run of decisions over three years tells you what the regulator actually prioritises, how it interprets an ambiguous provision in practice, how penalties are scaled, and where the enforcement frontier is moving — intelligence that no statement of policy provides as reliably as the decisions themselves.

#### Characteristic strengths

**Verifiability 5, structurally.** Determinations are published with reference numbers, legal bases, dates and reasoning. They can be cited exactly, retrieved by anyone, and checked against the register. Nothing else in the set is this stable — a vendor page changes silently, a news article moves, a decision keeps its citation.

**Consequences attach to being wrong.** A regulator that makes an unsupported finding faces appeal, judicial review, reversal, and institutional damage. That is a materially stronger discipline than reputational risk, and it is why the reasoning is usually laboured, qualified and evidenced to a standard other institutional sources do not attempt.

**Adversarially tested facts.** In contested proceedings the subject had counsel, an opportunity to respond, and every incentive to dispute the evidence. Facts that survive that process have been through a stress test no survey, article or white paper applies. **Findings from a contested decision are among the most reliable facts you will ever handle.**

**Procedural transparency.** Decisions state what standard was applied, what evidence was considered, what was excluded and why, and what the subject argued. As with the best Academic Authority work, disclosed method lets you interpret the finding rather than merely accept it — and lets you see precisely what was *not* decided.

#### Characteristic weaknesses

**Slow (Timeliness 1–3).** Investigations run for years; a decision published this quarter may concern conduct from four years ago, and the remedy may have been overtaken by events. Registers lag reality by whatever the filing deadline is. **A regulator's account of an organisation is a rear-view account, and for a fast-moving question it can be the oldest source in your pool while looking like the most authoritative.**

**Scope-bound to the remit.** The authority speaks only about what it regulates, only against the standard it administers, and only as far as its jurisdiction reaches. A data-protection regulator makes no finding about product quality. A financial supervisor is silent on labour practices. A national body has nothing to say about conduct abroad. **The finding is narrow even when the language sounds general, and the narrowness is invisible unless you read the legal basis.**

**Silence outside the remit is not a finding.** This is the most common misreading in the set, and it is worth stating as a rule: **absence of an enforcement action is not evidence of compliance.** It may mean the regulator has not looked, is under-resourced, is prioritising elsewhere, has a case open that is not yet public, or has no jurisdiction at all. "No adverse findings on the register" is a statement about the register, never about the subject. Write it that way.

**Capture and political constraint.** Regulators are institutions with budgets, appointment processes, and relationships with the sectors they oversee, and the revolving door between regulator and regulated is real. Capture shows up not as false findings but as selective attention — which sectors get inspected, which breaches get pursued, how penalties are calibrated against the size of the entity. **Read what a regulator does not investigate alongside what it decides.** Where a regulator is funded by fees from the regulated, treat it as sitting closer to Captured Expert on Independence and score accordingly.

**Compliance is a floor, not a description.** A determination that something is lawful says only that it met a minimum standard. It says nothing about whether it was competent, ethical, well-run or good. Sources routinely launder "approved" into "endorsed," and the two are unrelated.

#### Use cases in practice

**Credential and status verification.** Question: is the subject what it claims to be? Check the company register (does it exist, since when, who controls it, are the accounts filed or overdue), the relevant licence or authorisation register (current, lapsed, restricted, conditioned), and the enforcement history of the administering body. **Synthesis:** an entity's real age, control structure, standing and disciplinary record — often diverging sharply from the site's account of itself [Primary Artifact]. This check is cheap, and it should be run at INTEL on every named organisation in the pool.

**Extracting the compelled record.** Question: what actually went wrong inside the organisation? A published enforcement decision on an incident sets out the timeline the organisation supplied under compulsion, the internal controls found deficient, the number of people affected, and what the organisation told the regulator versus what it told the public. Cite the decision directly. Then take its specifics outward: does the disclosed timeline match the public statements from that period [Primary Artifacts]; did reporting at the time reflect the true scale [Independent Observer]. **You have obtained internal fact with no leak, no anonymity, and a citation that holds.**

**Mapping the enforcement frontier.** Question: what will actually be enforced? Read every decision the authority published in the area over three years. Note what conduct was pursued, what penalties were set against what turnover, which arguments the authority rejected, and which provisions have never been the basis of an action at all. **Synthesis:** the operative rule, as opposed to the written rule. **Caution:** the frontier moves, and three years of quiet on a provision does not predict a fourth — new leadership, new mandate, new priorities.

**When *not* to rely on a Regulatory Authority.** Question: what is happening in this market right now? The determination concerns 2022. The register was filed nine months ago. The inspection was two years back. For current state, use Primary Artifacts and Independent Observers, and use the regulator for the fixed points — the rules, the thresholds, the standing — around which the current picture is drawn. Equally, never use a regulator to answer a question outside its remit: the absence of an answer will read as a negative answer, and it is not one.

#### Red flags and cautions

- **A summary standing in for the determination.** A news article, an association bulletin, or an encyclopedia entry describing "what the ruling said" is a Tertiary Compiler or Independent Observer account of a document you can read yourself. Summaries routinely drop the scope limits, the conditions and the effective dates — precisely the parts that carry the meaning. **Retrieve the decision. Cite the decision.**
- **Draft, proposed, consulted-on, or appealed.** A consultation paper is not a rule. A proposed measure is not in force. A decision under appeal may be stayed, reduced or overturned. Check the procedural status before treating anything as settled, and state the status in the output.
- **"Registered," "notified," "filed" read as approval.** Many registers record what was submitted without assessing it, and many notification regimes involve no scrutiny whatsoever. Registration frequently means only that a form arrived. Establish what the register actually certifies before you rest anything on an entry.
- **Jurisdiction mismatch.** The finding is from one jurisdiction and your subject operates in another; the entity named in the decision is a differently-incorporated affiliate; the standard applied has since been replaced. All three are easy to miss and all three invalidate the inference.
- **The regulator as an interested party.** A body defending its own regime, its budget, or its record will frame findings accordingly — emphasising enforcement volume, characterising the regime as effective, and treating its own scope as adequate by assumption. The determinations remain reliable; the surrounding narrative, the annual report and the impact assessment are closer to a Captured Expert's output and should be read as such.

## Tagging drift — read this before you accept a default

A persona tag records what a source **actually is in this specific instance**. It is not a property of the platform, the domain, or the format. A media catalogue's default persona for a source type is a **starting guess, not an answer** — and a guess you are expected to overturn whenever the individual source does not fit it.

Two drift patterns are already visible in practice, and both are the same error:

**Unused personas.** Five of the defined twelve — Leaked Document, Academic Authority, Well-Meaning Generalist, Tertiary Compiler, Promotional Authority — are currently never applied in the catalogue at all. That is not evidence that no source of those kinds is ever collected. It is evidence that tagging is running off a narrow set of habitual defaults. If a persona never appears in your pool, ask whether it is genuinely absent from the sources you gathered or merely absent from the tags you applied.

**Misapplied defaults.** Peer-reviewed academic papers are being tagged **Captured Expert** rather than **Academic Authority**. Those are different sources with opposite Independence profiles — 5 against 2–3 — and the mistag silently corrupts every downstream judgement that rests on Independence. Capture is a finding about a *particular* piece of research: disclosed industry funding, a sponsor with a stake in the conclusion, a question shaped by who paid. It is not something you assume because a paper concerns a commercial subject.

**The rule:** tag by creator and intent, one source at a time. Do not tag by folder, by feed, by publisher, or by what the last similar-looking source was tagged. If a default and the source disagree, the source wins — and if the source could genuinely take two tags, tag it by who created it and why, then say in the CHECK note which alternative you rejected and on what grounds.

## Multi-persona strategies — use these at CHECK

Different questions require different combinations. If your pool doesn't resemble the right combination for your question, the pool is incomplete regardless of how many sources it holds.

**Question: is the subject genuinely prepared for a coming change?**
Primary Artifacts (hiring signals show stated intent) + Independent Observers (external assessment of whether the response is adequate) + Captured Experts (aggregated sector data, bias-adjusted) + Anonymous Insiders (internal readiness, pattern-based) + **Regulatory Authorities** (licence standing, enforcement history, filed compliance — what they were *required* to be ready for, and whether they were). Together they triangulate between aspiration and execution.

**Question: what solutions exist in the market?**
Promotional Insiders (vendor content showing real capability) + Promotional Authorities (analyst reports mapping the landscape) + Independent Observers (external assessment of competitive dynamics) + Primary Artifacts (vendor sites and product documentation confirming claimed capability). Promotional sources, read sceptically, map the landscape comprehensively; the others check it.

**Question: what is the cultural context shaping decisions?**
Academic Authorities (deep historical research on the values in play) + Independent Observers (current manifestations) + Well-Meaning Generalists (mainstream understanding and zeitgeist) + Captured Experts (surveys revealing shared assumptions inside the sector).

**Question: is there a gap between what they say and what they do?**
Primary Artifacts (the stated position) + Primary Artifacts of a different type (the operational artifacts — hiring, spending, structure) + Anonymous Insiders (lived internal experience, pattern-based) + Independent Observers (external verification) + **Regulatory Authorities** (a determination is the gap established on the record, with consequences attached) + **Direct Witnesses** (named first-hand accounts of what actually happened in a specific instance). Note that the first two are the same persona used against itself, which is legitimate — but it does **not** satisfy the two-persona diversity gate on its own.

This is the question the two newest personas serve best. A gap between stated and actual is hard to establish from artifacts alone, because the subject controls the artifacts. A regulator's finding and a named witness's account are the two routes to the same fact that the subject does not control.

**Question: what actually happened in this specific instance?**
**Direct Witnesses** (named first-hand accounts, ideally more than one vantage point) + Primary Artifacts (contemporaneous records — timestamps, correspondence, logs) + Independent Observers (reporting that reconstructs the sequence) + **Regulatory Authorities** (where a determination exists, it fixes the record). Beware of resolving a disputed event from a single witness: one witness establishes a specific, never a pattern.
